Iran: Trump meets Qatar's mediator as Tehran says the next move is Washington's

What happened
  • 9 Nov (US time): Trump meets Qatari PM Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman Al Thani at the White House at noon Eastern (about 4am Tuesday AEDT). Vance is also due to meet him separately. Qatar denies reports that the US–Iran talks have failed.
  • Araghchi says Qatar-mediated talks produced a "two-day plan": the US takes agreed steps on day one and Iran reopens Hormuz on day two. Qatar's mediation cut it down from an earlier seven-day plan. Tehran wants sanctions relief and fewer US troops in the region. Araghchi's line is that it is now the White House's turn to choose.
  • Trump told CNN that new large-scale strikes are "highly unlikely" if talks progress, but he has three carriers in the region. He told CBS: "We would have had a deal except for this election." Rubio says the talks are "not productive" and Iran's demands are "simply unacceptable".
  • Xinhua, citing Axios, reports Vance has been in near-daily indirect contact with Tehran for two weeks through Qatar and Pakistan, and has proposed direct talks. Progress is described as very slow.
  • 5 Nov: Iran's security chief Mohsen Rezaei met Putin in the Kremlin and handed over a letter from Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei. Russia's military intelligence chief Igor Kostyukov also attended. Rezaei said Iran won't negotiate on ending the war or reopening Hormuz until the US meets seven conditions.
  • Also reported: a suspected Iranian drone attack halted Iraq's Khor Mor gas field (attribution unconfirmed). Iran's navy reports another tanker fire after a mine strike east of Hormuz.
Why it matters for Australia

Fuel is the most direct link. Australia imports almost all its liquid fuel as refined product from Asian refineries that depend on Gulf crude. Oil prices swung on Monday on Trump's and Rubio's mixed messages. A deal would ease pressure on prices and on the RBA. Failed talks plus three carriers could mean renewed strikes on power plants and bridges. Canberra has publicly urged de-escalation. Marles has tied any ADF role in Hormuz to a lasting ceasefire. The Qatar meeting is the clearest sign yet of whether that condition is getting closer.

State media framing · Tehran, Beijing and Moscow

Tehran presents itself as the patient party waiting on Washington. Tehran Times calls the midterms "a referendum on his presidency" that Trump lost because of the war. It calls the war "a political boomerang". The IRGC, via Press TV, says Hormuz is "fully under Iranian naval control" and warns of mines. Beijing's Xinhua Chinese-language channel reports the talks in detail and neutrally. It notes both Vance's diplomacy and Trump's list of power plants and bridges as targets, and avoids blaming either side. Moscow's TASS amplifies Trump's claim that the election cost him a deal. That casts Washington as divided. The Kremlin keeps its own role low-key while hosting Iran's security chief.

Angle to build

Both sides are already arguing over who walked away. Tehran blames US politics, and Trump blames the election. If talks fail, whose version will regional states and Asian oil importers accept? Also consider whether Russia's quiet role as Tehran's sounding board gives Moscow leverage in Washington that it can use over Ukraine.

Saudi–Houthi war escalates: village strike near Sanaa, missiles at Riyadh

What happened
  • 7 Nov: Airstrikes hit al-Shariya village in Bani Hushaysh, a Houthi stronghold east of Sanaa. Houthi-run Saba says at least 33 people were killed and 39 wounded, mostly women and children. Reuters could not verify the figures. Saudi Arabia's Center for International Communication says it is investigating the reports.
  • 8 Nov: The Saudi-led coalition says it intercepted a Houthi ballistic missile aimed at the Eastern Province and two missiles fired at Riyadh. Falling debris damaged an administrative building in Riyadh's Aramco Tower. The Energy Ministry reported limited damage and no casualties. The Houthis also claim attacks on Saudi airports and Aramco storage sites.
  • The fighting resumed in July and ended the 2022 UN-brokered truce. The IOM says more than 200,000 people have been displaced since then.
Why it matters for Australia

With Hormuz effectively shut, Saudi exports that bypass the strait (the East-West pipeline to Red Sea ports) matter more to the Asian refineries that supply Australia's fuel. Houthi strikes on Saudi energy sites and a Houthi naval blockade declared against Saudi Arabia threaten that bypass. A wider Gulf war also makes the region harder to stabilise even if a US–Iran deal is reached. That affects any future ADF maritime contribution.

State media framing · Tehran and Moscow

Tehran treats the Houthi strikes as justified retaliation. Tehran Times headlines that Yemen hit the Aramco tower "in response to Saudi attacks". Iran's foreign ministry condemned the Saudi airstrikes and called for them to end, without mentioning Houthi missile fire. Moscow's RT gave prominence to the damage at Aramco Tower and to Saudi civilian casualties. The milblogger channel Rybar claims "Ukrainian UAVs" were shot down over Yemen (unverified). That fits a wider Russian line that ties Kyiv to every war.

Angle to build

Is Iran using the Houthis to pressure Riyadh while it bargains with Washington through Qatar? Is the Saudi–Houthi war now a separate conflict that a US–Iran deal would not end? Your answer shapes how much a Hormuz deal would really ease Australia's fuel risk.

Russia pounds Ukraine's grid, reportedly with North Korean missiles, before Abu Dhabi talks

What happened
  • 7–8 Nov: Russian strikes cut power to more than 600,000 people in Dnipropetrovsk region (DTEK) and about 250,000 in Kharkiv region. The Energy Ministry says the 7 Nov attack hit five regions and disrupted transmission to Kyiv.
  • 9 Nov: Zelensky says preliminary findings show Russia used North Korean KN-23 ballistic missiles on a key energy facility. This is the first time he has named the model in this campaign. He asked allies for more interceptors and tougher sanctions.
  • Russia's MoD says it will keep striking fuel and energy facilities it says support Ukraine's military. It claims 430 Ukrainian drones were downed overnight. DPR head Pushilin claims advances along the Donetsk front near Druzhkovka (unverified).
  • US-brokered US–Russia–Ukraine talks are due in Abu Dhabi on 10–11 Nov, with Witkoff and Kushner leading for the US.
  • Deputy FM Pankin told TASS that a US–Russia "diesel deal" shows "opportunities remain" to restore business ties. He called it a temporary exemption, not sanctions relief. Merz says he wouldn't rule out meeting Putin at the Miami G20.
Why it matters for Australia

KN-23s used against Ukraine show Indo-Pacific and European security are linked. Combat use helps Pyongyang refine missiles that threaten Japan and South Korea. Australia has sanctioned Russia and given military aid to Ukraine. If Washington offers Moscow sanctions exemptions, Canberra will have to decide whether its own autonomous sanctions follow.

State media framing · Moscow

Moscow describes the strikes as military targeting, not pressure before the talks. The MoD says it hit facilities that "support the Ukrainian military". TASS frames the diplomacy as a US–Russia thaw: Pankin's diesel deal and Merz's openness to meeting Putin signal that the West is coming back to the table. The Russian outlets in the digests did not address the KN-23 claim.

Angle to build

Moscow is pairing grid strikes with signals of a thaw. Is it trying to make Washington see sanctions relief as the price of a ceasefire? If US exemptions grow, what is the cost to Australia's credibility of keeping sanctions that Washington has relaxed?

China's DF-61 ICBM moves fast into routine training

What happened
  • 5 Nov: CCTV reported that the DF-61 road-mobile ICBM, first shown at the September 2025 parade, is in routine PLA Rocket Force training. The training covers field mobility, moving between launch positions and launch procedures. The report marked 66 years since the DF-1 launch. State media also showed the rarely seen DF-100 cruise missile.
  • CCTV gave no range, numbers or basing. Training does not prove full operational readiness.
  • Social media claims of an inland ICBM test, possibly including a mid-course interception, are unconfirmed (SCMP).
  • SCMP analysts also ask whether recent PLA Navy deployments in the western Pacific and South China Sea rehearse keeping US forces out of a Taiwan conflict.
  • Background: In September 2024 China fired a DF-31B ICBM into the South Pacific. Reports say it fired a submarine-launched missile into the Pacific in July 2026. The US and allies want 24 hours' notice of such launches.
Why it matters for Australia

China's fast nuclear build-up underpins its effort to deter the US from intervening over Taiwan, the scenario that would test the alliance and AUKUS. Australia is within ICBM range, and recent PLA tests have landed in the South Pacific near Pacific partners. ASPI argues the PLA Southern Theater Command matters most to Australia. A larger strategic nuclear force covers its conventional operations closer to home.

State media framing · Beijing

Global Times presents the DF-61 as routine force modernisation and deterrence. It quotes commentator Shao Yongling calling it "an upgrade to China's land-based nuclear counterstrike capabilities". He also says it shows "a high level of technological maturity". The coverage names no adversary. Former ambassador Cui Tiankai says the US "must stop blaming China" for its domestic problems (SCMP). No Chinese statement on Australia, AUKUS or the Quad was found this period.

Angle to build

Beijing publicises its nuclear progress while staying quiet on arms control and launch notification. Ask whether Australia should press for missile-launch notification through the Pacific Islands Forum and the Quad, where its interests are most direct.

North Korea tests new missiles and wins new embassies as its missiles appear in Ukraine

What happened
  • 5 Nov: KCNA said the Missile Administration test-fired a new surface-to-air missile system, overseen by Gen. Kim Jong Sik. Separately, a strategic cruise missile unit hit a simulated airport target. This followed a reported cluster-type cruise missile warhead test on 2 Nov. The performance claims are not independently confirmed.
  • Belarus has opened an embassy in Pyongyang, and Brazil's first ambassador in a decade has taken up his post (NK News).
  • North Korean loudspeakers are visible at the border again, but no broadcasts have been heard.
  • 9 Nov: Zelensky says Russia used KN-23s on Ukraine's grid (see Europe story).
  • Australia's latest move is not new: on 22 Oct it sanctioned Yu Pu Ung, Sim Hyon Sop and two IT firms over fake remote-work schemes that fund weapons programs. The US has since blacklisted a Seychelles-based crypto exchange over North Korean money laundering.
Why it matters for Australia

Pyongyang is testing air defences and strikes on airfields, the kind of targets that matter to US bases in Japan and South Korea. Australia relies on those bases in a regional crisis. North Korean IT workers have used stolen Australian identity documents to win remote jobs, so the cyber and sanctions threat reaches Australian firms directly. More embassies in Pyongyang mean its isolation is easing.

State media framing · Pyongyang

KCNA presents the tests as routine checks of design and technical specifications. It says the cruise missile unit completed its mission. KCNA did not link the tests to any foreign event. No North Korean statement on Australia's sanctions, AUKUS or the Quad was found. Pyongyang does not publicly confirm that its missiles are used in Ukraine.

Angle to build

Each KN-23 fired in Ukraine gives Pyongyang combat data and gives Moscow reasons to share technology. Should Australia frame North Korea in public as a Euro-Atlantic threat as well as a Korean one? Would that help or hurt in getting Southeast Asian partners to enforce sanctions?
