Xi hosts Trump and Putin for the first three-way summit while Albanese waits for his turn

What happened
  • The leaders of the US, China and Russia are due to meet together today (19 November) on the APEC sidelines in Shenzhen. It is the first meeting of its kind.
  • The White House lists "the reduction of nuclear weapons in the world" on the agenda. A senior US official said: "We do not expect to get to any kind of agreement." Iran and Ukraine are also expected to come up. Ukraine's place in the talks is still unclear.
  • Kremlin aide Ushakov says a separate Putin–Trump bilateral will follow the plenary.
  • Xi broke precedent by greeting Trump on the tarmac on 18 November. Putin and Xi held more than two hours of talks the same day and pledged a "fairer multipolar world order". It was their fifth meeting this year.
  • Albanese landed in Shenzhen on 18 November. His one-on-one with Xi is set for 20 November, followed by the annual leaders' meeting with Premier Li Qiang in Beijing. He said "the relationship with China is strong".
  • Xi told South Korea's Lee Jae Myung he supports renewed US–North Korea dialogue. Seoul and Beijing say they have "substantially concluded" the second phase of their FTA talks.
  • No summit outcomes had been reported at the time of writing.
Why it matters for Australia

This is a summit of the three nuclear superpowers, held in Australia's region, and US allies are not in the room. Any deal or understanding on nuclear arms, Taiwan, Iran or Ukraine would shape Australia's strategic setting without Canberra having a say. Albanese's meeting with Xi tomorrow will be read against whatever Trump and Xi agree today. Trade, the Darwin Port and PLA activity are likely topics.

State media framing · Beijing and Moscow

Both capitals are running the summit as a world-order event. Ma Hui of the CPC International Department told TASS "the very fact that this is happening is a significant event". TASS reports Xi called the China–Russia relationship a "model for a new type of relationship between great powers". Putin told the APEC CEO Summit that "former hegemons" are resisting a fairer order. RT ran Russian experts calling it "an absolutely unprecedented event". The same RT piece played down the Yalta comparison: Vasily Kashin said Yalta parallels "have nothing to do with the real agenda", and argued Britain and France "must be brought into the same system" on nuclear limits. Xinhua and Global Times frame APEC around Xi's call to build an Asia-Pacific community "to prosper together". Neither has said anything about Australia's role.

Angle to build

Is a US–China–Russia format a stabilising arms-control channel or a 'concert of great powers' that sidelines middle powers like Australia? Test it against Kashin's point: Moscow wants US allies' nuclear forces counted. That logic could later reach AUKUS and extended deterrence.

US–Iran fighting flares in the Gulf: Qeshm strike, drones on Kuwait and Bahrain, ships hit near Hormuz

What happened
  • 17 Nov: US jets struck an IRGC missile launch site on Qeshm Island. A US official called it "self-defense" after Iran targeted a US-operated radar at Bahrain's Sheikh Isa Air Base. US officials say this was Iran's third attempted strike on a US installation in under a week, and that all were intercepted.
  • 18 Nov: Bahrain and Kuwait say they intercepted Iranian drone attacks. Iran's Army claims hits on Patriot systems at Ali Al Salem, Kuwait. CENTCOM says no US personnel were injured. Iran's damage claims are unconfirmed.
  • An ADNOC ship was struck near Hormuz. One crew member is missing and 11 are injured. Iranian outlets also claim that a third and then a fourth tanker hit mines while "violating" Tehran's rules. Those mine claims are unconfirmed.
  • Iran's Army says it shot down "one more" US MQ-9 east of Hormuz. This is unconfirmed.
  • The USS Theodore Roosevelt's arrival gives the US three carrier strike groups in the region. US officials describe this as temporary.
  • Before arriving in China, Trump warned Iran it would "pay a price the likes of which no country has ever paid" if a reported plot on US soil went ahead.
Why it matters for Australia

Fuel is the most direct exposure. Australia imports most of its refined fuel from Asian refineries that depend on Gulf crude. Albanese has raised the war's cost-of-living impact with Trump and at the Quad. Protesters in Shenzhen held placards critical of Australia's part in the Iran war. The digests also flag an Iran International report, not confirmed elsewhere, of a Quds Force-directed plot against Israel's embassy in Bangkok. That would matter for Southeast Asian threat assessments.

State media framing · Tehran and Beijing

Tehran casts the minings as law enforcement and the strikes as success. IRNA says mined tankers were violating "regulations set in place by Tehran". Press TV says Iran has "firm legal grounds" to control Hormuz. IRGC spokesman Mohebbi mocked CENTCOM over losses of "expensive equipment". Army chief Hatami warns of a response "without geographical limitations". Speaker Qalibaf says CNN's dirty-bomb report aims to "manufacture a pretext for war". Araghchi is pushing "indigenous security" for West Asia, with no outside powers. Beijing is relaying without endorsing: Xinhua's Telegram passed on Iran's Kuwait claim without comment. CGTN led with the US strike on Qeshm and Vance's "forever war" remarks.

Angle to build

Iran is building a legal narrative for 'regulating' Hormuz. Watch how that interacts with freedom-of-navigation principles Australia defends in the South China Sea. Does Beijing's careful neutrality here tell you anything about how it would treat a chokepoint it controls?

Canberra and Manila renew defence pact as the Quad adds maritime surveillance; Beijing shrugs

What happened
  • On 15 November, Philippine Defence Secretary Gilberto Teodoro and Australian Ambassador Marc Innes-Brown signed an updated Defence Cooperation Arrangement in Manila. It marks 80 years of diplomatic relations. One report says Albanese witnessed it.
  • Teodoro described it as the mechanism that makes the Status of Visiting Forces Agreement work, which points to procedural streamlining rather than new capabilities. Reuters-based coverage framed it as fast-tracking cooperation and bolstering deterrence.
  • Marles said in 2025 that Australia is pursuing eight infrastructure projects at five locations in the Philippines.
  • The Quad leaders met in Manila for the first time in two years. They agreed an "Indo-Pacific Maritime Surveillance Collaboration", and the first Quad maritime exercise, with P-8 and P-3C aircraft, is planned for next year. Australia also signed the 10-nation "San Francisco Declaration" on protecting sensitive technology.
  • Japan's defence ministry reports that the carrier Liaoning is on an extended western Pacific deployment, supported by replenishment ships.
Why it matters for Australia

The Philippines sits on the South China Sea frontline, and Australia is building a real military presence there. A smoother visiting-forces process makes ADF rotations and exercises easier. The Quad surveillance work builds on the P-8 fleet the RAAF already flies. Whether either step adds real deterrence or just process is still an open question.

State media framing · Beijing

Global Times dismissed the Manila Quad meeting as "photo diplomacy", according to the Source Digest. It quoted observers saying the "loose grouping" cannot shape the region's path. Separate reporting says Global Times called the Quad a "small circle" revived by Washington. I could not access the original Global Times text to confirm the exact wording. I found no Chinese state media statement on the Australia–Philippines arrangement. People's Daily instead ran a Fijian journalist praising APEC cooperation, a Pacific voice placed behind Beijing's message.

Angle to build

Beijing's line is that Shenzhen is where regional order is set and the Quad is a sideshow. Is it the volume of agreements or the actual capability behind them (aircraft, bases, sea days) that should count in judging whether that framing is wrong?

ASPI warns the Collins submarine refurbishment is overrunning before the AUKUS boats arrive

What happened
  • Writing in The Strategist on 18 November, retired submariner Peter Briggs says Rankin's full-cycle docking at Osborne is running over time. The delay started with problems on Sheehan, the boat before it.
  • Farncomb is waiting on the hardstand until Rankin leaves the hall. Briggs says its return to the fleet by June 2028 looks unlikely.
  • HMAS Collins must begin its life-of-type extension by June 2028.
  • In the normal cycle, three of the six boats are available. With two boats in full-cycle docking and two in lighter maintenance, only two would be available.
  • Collins sustainment has been a Project of Concern since December 2024, and will stay one until at least October 2028.
  • ASC's Collins work is competing for Osborne workers with the Hunter-class frigate build and with SSN-AUKUS, which will eventually need 4,000–5,500 shipyard workers.
  • The ANAO's May 2026 audit had already found an average 12-month delay against key design milestones. It also found that four of the five key design reviews had missed their schedule.
  • This is an analyst's assessment. Defence has not published updated availability figures.
Why it matters for Australia

Collins is Australia's only submarine force until the Virginia-class boats arrive in the 2030s. Fewer boats at sea means fewer training days, and that feeds a crewing problem. That matters because nuclear crews will come from the Collins fleet. A capability gap undercuts the deterrence case for AUKUS.

State media framing · Beijing

I found no Chinese, Russian, Iranian or North Korean state media statement on the Collins overrun in this period. Beijing's general line on AUKUS is that it fuels an arms race and threatens non-proliferation. Expect any delays to be used to argue that AUKUS is costly and unachievable.

Angle to build

Is the real constraint on AUKUS the US and UK industrial base, or Australia's own workforce at Osborne? How would you measure the 'capability gap' risk in sea days rather than hull numbers?

Crimean Bridge hit and French troops confirmed in Ukraine; Moscow widens its list of 'parties to the war'

What happened
  • Ukraine struck the Crimean Bridge on 17 November. TASS says rail traffic has fully resumed, and Deputy PM Nikitin says the bridge can be restored "in a short time". The full extent of the damage is unconfirmed. Milblogger Rybar wrote of its "destruction".
  • The Moscow Times reports Russia is asking whether the Netherlands is now a party to the war, because a Dutch company's missiles were reportedly used.
  • Paris has confirmed a small French troop presence in Ukraine for training and repairs. Russia says the troops are legitimate targets.
  • Russia's MoD claims a "massive strike" on Ukraine's defence industry and hits on Izmail port. TASS says Kyiv's Severny Bridge is damaged.
  • Rybar concedes a "threatening situation" for Russian forces in the Sumy direction.
  • US Under Secretary Colby said Ukraine's deep strikes may not have helped. Germany's defence minister called that "quite a stupid thing".
Why it matters for Australia

Australia supplies Ukraine, including Bushmasters and Abrams tanks. Russia now treats Western suppliers and trainers as possible targets, and that sets precedents for how adversaries view third-party support in any Indo-Pacific conflict. Any Ukraine terms traded at Shenzhen would also test Australia's sanctions policy.

State media framing · Moscow

Moscow is treating each new Western role as an escalation and each Western disagreement as fatigue. TASS played up French opposition calls to stay out of the war, a 12% approval rating for Merz, and Portugal refusing to send F-16s. Lavrov likened Europe's proposed negotiators to a dysfunctional orchestra from a fable. RT says a US official rebuked Kyiv for "spectacle" strikes. On the bridge, TASS stresses fast repair and "fully resumed" rail traffic.

Angle to build

Moscow is putting Washington–Kyiv friction alongside the Shenzhen summit. Is the Kremlin trying to split Europe from the US on Ukraine at the very moment it sits down with Trump, and would a split change Europe's interest in the Indo-Pacific?

Trump–Kim talk grows as Pyongyang leans on Moscow and keeps enriching

What happened
  • Rubio says Trump is "serious" about meeting Kim Jong Un but did not confirm any talks. Trump has said a meeting during this trip would be "up to" Xi.
  • Xi told Lee Jae Myung he supports renewed US–North Korea dialogue.
  • KCNA, via Yonhap, reports Putin met Kim's top aide Cho Yong-won and praised "decades of combat friendship". Russia will unveil a monument this year to North Korean troops' role in Kursk.
  • France 24 reports the IAEA says North Korea has finished the outside works on an extension to an enrichment facility. I could not find the IAEA statement itself. In September, the IAEA identified a new enrichment building at Yongbyon.
  • China–North Korea trade fell to US$254 million in October, despite a new bridge opening.
Why it matters for Australia

Any Trump–Kim deal that accepts North Korea's nuclear status would change extended deterrence in Northeast Asia, including for Japan and South Korea, two key partners for Australia. ASPI argues the goal should shift from denuclearisation to managing the threat. Australia also helps enforce UN sanctions on North Korea at sea.

State media framing · Pyongyang and Beijing

KCNA material in this period has not reacted to Trump's overtures. It stresses ties with Russia instead: Cho's meeting with Putin and the Kursk monument. That suggests Pyongyang wants to show it has other options. No direct KCNA output came through the digests in the last 12 hours. Beijing presents itself as the facilitator: Xi "supports" dialogue, and CGTN quickly amplified Rubio's remarks.

Angle to build

If Xi is the broker of a Trump–Kim meeting, what does Beijing collect in return, and does that price fall on Taiwan or on the US alliance system Australia relies on?
