Xi hails a "full turnaround" with Australia; the Shenzhen trilateral delivers style over substance

What happened
  • Xi met Albanese on Friday 20 November on the APEC sidelines in Shenzhen. It was their fifth formal meeting since 2022.
  • Xi said: "We have jointly promoted the full turnaround of China-Australia relations." He called Albanese a "China hand" and agreed to make 2027 the Australia–China Year of Tourism.
  • Albanese called the talks "constructive, friendly and engaging". He said the relationship is not "defined by our differences" but still needs frank conversations.
  • The ABC reports that iron ore pricing, beef quotas and Darwin Port remain unresolved. Exporters hit with 55 per cent tariffs after exceeding the beef quota are still waiting. Albanese goes on to Beijing to meet Premier Li Qiang and a business roundtable.
  • The day before, the Xi–Trump–Putin "declaration of peace" covered UN reform, nuclear risk and a warning to the ICC. It left out Ukraine and Iran, and the three could not agree on future nuclear talks. Finland's President Stubb says a Ukraine clause was dropped at US request.
  • In his APEC speech, with Putin in the room, Albanese said: "end your war, end it now."
Why it matters for Australia

China takes about a quarter of Australian exports, so a stable relationship has real economic value. But the trilateral format, three permanent Security Council members setting the agenda for "strategic stability", leaves out middle powers like Australia. A great-power bargain struck over allies' heads, on Ukraine or Taiwan, is the scenario Canberra's alliance strategy is meant to prevent. The ICC language also cuts against Australia's backing for international courts, which Wong restated this week after the ICJ's Rohingya genocide ruling.

State media framing · Beijing

Chinese state media presented Xi as the convenor of a new order. Global Times called the trilateral a "landmark diplomatic event to go down in history". Wang Yi called APEC Shenzhen "a new peak" in Asia-Pacific cooperation. On Australia, Xinhua and CGTN carried Xi's call for "mutually trusted partners" and a relationship "developing steadily". Global Times ran a positive piece on resources ties through "industrial upgrading". None of the state media coverage we saw mentioned AUKUS, the Quad or Darwin Port. Moscow's TASS and RT framed the summit as Europe being sidelined, citing a Bloomberg column on "a final humiliation to Europe".

Angle to build

Is Beijing's "full turnaround" line a reward for Canberra staying quiet on security issues? If Australia pushes back on AUKUS or Taiwan, does the warmth become conditional? Compare China's treatment of Japan's Takaichi this same week.

Russia's "painful" response: mass strike on Ukraine, missiles into Poland, Europe split on talks

What happened
  • Overnight into 20 November, Russia launched one of its largest strikes of the war. Ukraine's air force counted 84 cruise missiles, an unspecified number of ballistic missiles and more than 400 drones. Eight people were killed.
  • Three bridges over the Dnipro in Kyiv were hit, the biggest such attack since February 2022. A water utility and power infrastructure were also struck, causing blackouts. Two people died at a Lviv railway station.
  • Poland's defence minister says three objects, probably two missiles and a drone, violated Polish airspace. Warsaw called it a "provocation". Trump called them "a couple of little missiles, by mistake".
  • Putin says the Crimean Bridge is a civilian structure. He has ruled out a maritime ceasefire or a halt to energy strikes.
  • Estonia will close all road crossings with Russia from 1 December, and Denmark is expelling Russia's military attaché. Meanwhile Germany's Merz and Pistorius are pushing for European talks in Moscow.
  • The FT reports, via TASS, that Washington wants a peace deal before early January, when Democrats take control of Congress.
Why it matters for Australia

Australia has given Ukraine military aid and sanctioned Russia, and Albanese confronted Putin to his face this week. A US push for a fast deal that leaves out Europe and Kyiv would test whether allies have a real say in Washington's bargains. Australian planners will draw the lesson for the Indo-Pacific. Poland's mild treatment from Washington also signals how Trump reads allied airspace violations.

State media framing · Moscow

MoD Russia called the strike a "massive strike" on defence industry, energy and transport targets. Russian state channels in the digests did not mention the missiles that landed in Poland. Moscow is building a case that the Netherlands is a co-belligerent: RT ran an "investigation" alleging Dutch firm Destinus helped with the bridge strike, and Zakharova criticised its "complicity". TASS pushed the line that "Kyiv is not interested in peace". Medvedev called a NATO-for-territory idea "insane". Moscow called Estonia's border closure a new "Iron Curtain". The milblogger Rybar quoted an expert dismissing the barrage as "routine operations", not the promised retaliation.

Angle to build

Moscow blames Kyiv for stalled peace while praising Trump's deal-making and isolating hawkish Europeans. Does this aim to split Washington from Europe, and Berlin from the Baltics and Nordics? What would an Australian equivalent of that wedge look like?

Iran moves to run the US blockade as mine claims mount and Washington weighs new strikes

What happened
  • The IRGC Navy says a tanker on an "unauthorised route" through Hormuz struck mines near Khasab. This follows claims that two more tankers hit mines on 19 November. CBS notes that few such claims are ever corroborated. UKMTO separately confirmed a tanker was hit by an unknown projectile north of Khasab on 18 November.
  • Araghchi told ILNA "the blockade must be broken". The Guards-affiliated Fars agency urged a tanker convoy and threatened strikes on oil facilities in the UAE, Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Kuwait, Oman and Bahrain if Iranian tankers are attacked. A convoy to China is unconfirmed.
  • CENTCOM widened the blockade to ship-to-ship transfers on 18 November. Kpler recorded no Iranian loadings in September.
  • Trump says there are indications Iran is "looking at" biological and chemical weapons. US and Israeli officials disagree on how strong the intelligence is. The NYT reports Netanyahu wants a new four-to-seven-day strike campaign. Trump says "I'm not rushing" and will decide after the new year.
  • At the IAEA Board, Tehran ruled out unrestricted inspections while it is "under threat". Senate Republicans blocked a war powers resolution. A Pentagon official put the war's cost at US$48.3 billion as of 13 November.
Why it matters for Australia

Australia imports almost all its refined fuel, much of it from Asian refineries that depend on Gulf crude. Mid-year reporting put fuel stocks well below the 90-day target, at 46 days of petrol and 39 of diesel in July. We have no newer figures. A wider war on Gulf energy sites would hit fuel prices, inflation and ADF logistics. Any new US campaign could also bring alliance requests for support.

State media framing · Tehran, echoed by Moscow and Beijing

Tehran presents the mine strikes as enforcing "strait regulations" against "non-compliant" ships, building the idea of an Iranian permit regime. Baqaei called the weapons claims a "big lie" following "the same fraudulent script" as Iraq. TASS relayed that line almost word for word. IRGC chief Vahidi says Iranian naval dominance reaches 500 km beyond the Gulf, and that the enemy must "admit defeat and withdraw". Leader's adviser Mokhber says Shenzhen proved Washington can no longer build a coalition against Iran. Xinhua relayed the IRGC mine claim and the Pentagon cost figure without comment.

Angle to build

Iran is trying to make a mined, permit-based strait look normal. China relays this without endorsing it, while Russia amplifies it. Which matters more for Asian fuel buyers like Australia: the military balance in the strait, or whether insurers and shipping lines accept Iran's rules?

Russian bombers probe Japan and Taiwan as Beijing presses Tokyo and Washington on Taiwan

What happened
  • Japan says suspected Russian aircraft violated its airspace on 17 November, the first such violation since September 2024. The aircraft passed through the Tsushima Strait, flew between Yonaguni and Taiwan, and went out into the Philippine Sea. Defence Minister Koizumi called it a "serious infringement" of sovereignty.
  • Taiwan says two Russian military aircraft entered its air defence identification zone. TWZ reports that two Tu-95s and two Il-76s later landed in Da Nang, Vietnam. Neither Tokyo nor Taipei has officially confirmed the aircraft types.
  • The Diplomat reports that Takaichi met Putin in Shenzhen the next day.
  • SCMP reports Xi told Trump the US "must keep doing the right thing" on Taiwan, which analysts read as pressure over arms sales. PLA Navy officers also held maritime security talks with INDOPACOM officials in Shanghai.
  • Taiwan's TAIEX reportedly fell almost 18 per cent this week, which Taipei Times calls its worst week since 1991. We could not confirm this independently. Taiwan has also told Meta about China-linked accounts targeting its elections.
Why it matters for Australia

Russian long-range flights along the first island chain show that Moscow can now operate in Australia's main area of strategic interest alongside China. ASPI urges Canberra and its partners to plan for that. If Washington makes arms-sales concessions on Taiwan as part of a bargain with Beijing, the regional deterrence that AUKUS is designed to support would weaken.

State media framing · Beijing and Moscow

Neither Russian nor Chinese state media in the digests mentioned the bomber flights. Both focused on summit diplomacy. Global Times said Taiwan came up in China–US talks and claimed "anxiety mounts among island separatist forces". Beijing is targeting Japan: Global Times says Takaichi must retract her "survival-threatening situation" remarks and that Japan's failed bid for a formal US meeting shows "diplomatic anxiety". People's Daily highlighted record-high negative South Korean views of the US.

Angle to build

Beijing's line is that America's allies are anxious and drifting away. It is aimed at Japan and South Korea but not at Australia. Why the difference, and how long will Australia be spared?

Sydney man charged with terror offences after raids across the south-west

What happened
  • The NSW Joint Counter Terrorism Team raided properties in about 10 Sydney suburbs on 20 November.
  • Nowroz Amin, 35, was arrested at Ingleburn. He is charged with making a document connected with terrorism and using a carriage service to possess violent extremist material. Police allege he researched how to build a "vehicle-borne explosive device". The material allegedly relates to religiously motivated extremism, including Islamic State. Bail was formally refused at Campbelltown Local Court.
  • Two other men were charged with unrelated offences: hindering police, and firearms offences.
  • AFP Deputy Commissioner Hilda Sirec said there is no ongoing threat, the man is believed to have acted alone, and no targets were identified. Further charges have not been ruled out.
Why it matters for Australia

Australia's national terrorism threat level was raised to "probable" in 2024. This case fits ASIO's warnings about lone actors radicalised online. With the Middle East wars driving grievance narratives, expect pressure on social cohesion and on online-harm laws.

State media framing · None (domestic)

We found no Chinese, Russian, Iranian or North Korean state media statement on this case.

Angle to build

How do the Gaza and Iran wars shape radicalisation risk in Australia? Do current counter-terrorism laws fit lone actors who research but have not picked a target?
