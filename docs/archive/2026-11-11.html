Iran claims three more tanker blasts in Hormuz, shows off a 'missile city' and denies any talks

What happened
  • 10 Nov: The IRGC Navy said two "offending" tankers exploded on an "unauthorised" route south of the Strait of Hormuz. It then released footage of a third tanker that it says hit a naval mine. It accused the US military of luring tankers into the strait. These claims are unconfirmed: maritime agencies have not verified the IRGC's recent mine-strike announcements, though UKMTO did report two tankers hit or on fire on 7 Nov.
  • Iranian state TV says 11 tankers were attacked in 10 days. The IRGC denies the US claim that the mines have been cleared. A UKMTO weekly count to 10 Nov put transits about 65% below pre-war levels.
  • 10 Nov: The IRGC Aerospace Force unveiled a new underground missile base. Armed forces chief Maj. Gen. Abdollahi promised "surprises in the next war".
  • 10 Nov: Security chief Mohsen Rezaei posted that Washington should stop inventing "imaginary talks" and accept Iran's seven conditions. Per Al Jazeera, these include lifting the US blockade of Iran's southern ports and lifting sanctions. FM Araghchi denied any planned Rezaei–Vance meeting in Doha, which Turkey's intelligence chief had said Qatar would host next week.
  • The other track: Qatar's PM met Trump on 9 Nov and called the talks positive. Trump called the war "effectively over" militarily but said the blockade stays. The Jerusalem Post reports he gave Vance "a few days" to reach a deal. Rubio says a breakthrough is not imminent.
  • Spillover: Saudi Arabia confirmed a new attack on its East-West oil pipeline, the main bypass around Hormuz (reported via RT; the Houthis claimed it). Xinhua reported an ADNOC vessel damaged in UAE waters.
  • I could not confirm a fresh Brent price for 10 Nov. It was about US$94 at yesterday's brief.
Why it matters for Australia

This is the fuel story. Australia imports most of its refined fuel from Asian refiners that run on Gulf crude, so a strait running at a third of normal traffic keeps petrol, diesel and freight prices high here. The attack on the Saudi bypass pipeline matters because it is the main way around Hormuz. Albanese said on 7 Nov he has told Trump "publicly and directly" that it is time to de-escalate. A deal within Trump's "few days" would ease pressure fast; a collapse brings back Trump's threat to hit 38 bridges, and with it a new price spike.

State media framing · Tehran, echoed by Moscow

Tehran's line is that it holds the stronger hand and Washington is the one who needs talks. Rezaei, carried by Press TV and Tehran Times: "FOLD YOUR HAND. STOP manufacturing FAKE NEWS about imaginary talks" and "Time to SURRENDER to the seven conditions". He called US claims of Qatar talks a "pure lie" meant to lower oil prices. Mehr argues Hormuz has "exposed the limits of US military power". The tankers are framed as lawbreakers, not victims: "offending" ships on an "unauthorised" route. Speaker Qalibaf on the missile base: Iran's forces "will dictate the geography of the war". Yet IRNA also ran Qatar's report of "positive signals" from both sides, so the door is not shut. TASS and RT repeat Rezaei's lines largely without challenge. Xinhua reports the ADNOC vessel attack flatly, without Tehran's framing. None mentioned Australia.

Angle to build

Tehran denies talks loudly while its own news agency carries Qatar's "positive signals". Is the denial aimed at Washington (to raise the price), at oil markets (to keep prices up), or at hardliners at home? Test each reading against what Iran does in the strait over the next few days.

Abu Dhabi day one: no breakthrough, and Moscow wants to talk about Europe before Ukraine

What happened
  • 10 Nov: Russian, Ukrainian and US delegations met in Abu Dhabi for more than four hours. It was the first trilateral round since February. A second day is confirmed for 11 Nov and the Russian side has promised a press conference.
  • Who: Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff for the US; Rustem Umerov leads for Ukraine, with Kyrylo Budanov in the team; Vladimir Medinsky leads an 11-person Russian team including deputy foreign ministers Galuzin and Ryabkov. Meduza reports it also includes an FSB officer whom Ukraine accuses of trying to kill Budanov.
  • Topics, per ABC and Ukrainian media: security guarantees, territory, prisoners (a large exchange may be announced), reconstruction and a Europe-wide security framework.
  • The split on sequencing: a Russian delegation source told Reuters the Ukraine settlement is "just one aspect" of a wider European security discussion. Lavrov wants that framework agreed first and rejects a truce. Von der Leyen says wider talks come after a ceasefire.
  • Europe has no seat. EU, NATO, French, German and British officials are in Abu Dhabi and met the Ukrainians and Americans separately. Rubio says the aim is to widen the format to Europeans later.
  • Reported, not confirmed: The Economist says the US floated redrawing Donetsk and Luhansk borders so Russia could claim full control. A US official told Reuters each side might cede a little territory.
  • Reactions: Rubio called day one "very productive" with "a lot of work" left. Zelenskyy called it "constructive" but said "it's hard to deal with the Russians". Separately, RT reports the European Council decided to send EU military advisers to Ukraine.
Why it matters for Australia

Australia has given Ukraine more than A$1.5b in support and runs sanctions against Russia, so the terms of any deal matter here. The bigger issue is precedent. A settlement that rewards seizing territory, or lets great powers redesign a region's security order over the heads of the states in it, will be read closely in Beijing and across the Indo-Pacific. It also shows how Washington treats allies when it wants a deal: Europe is in the building but not in the room. And if a deal is close, expect pressure on partners like Australia over sanctions relief and reconstruction money.

State media framing · Moscow and Beijing

Moscow says little on substance and a lot on format. Peskov: "We will not discuss any models publicly". A TASS source casts Ukraine as one item in talks on a post-conflict "continental security system", in a format that excludes Europe. Peskov on a European seat: "the Americans have their own channels of communication with the Europeans". Europe is cast as the spoiler: TASS quoted an Italian analyst calling Ukraine a "black hole" for an EU that "tried to sabotage talks", and RT repeated that Western troops in Ukraine would be legitimate targets. A Russian envoy told TASS Ukraine fired more than 8,200 munitions at Russia in a week "ahead of talks". Rybar says results will stem from "ground realities". Beijing stays on the sidelines but echoes the Europe-is-divided theme: Xinhua called a European exercise a "bid for role" and highlighted Czech and Slovak resistance to funding Kyiv. None mentioned Australia.

Angle to build

Moscow is using a Ukraine negotiation to reopen the question it lost in December 2021: who sets Europe's security order. If Washington agrees to discuss that without Europeans present, what does it tell US allies in Asia about being consulted on a future US–China bargain?

The Iran war reaches allied homelands: France blames the IRGC for the fridge hacks; US planes leave a second UK base

What happened
  • 9–10 Nov: France formally blamed the IRGC for cyberattacks on 11–12 Oct that overheated fridges and freezers at about 700 supermarkets and other businesses. Some stores lost all fresh and frozen stock. A French intelligence report named the IRGC cyber unit Shahid Kaveh and three alleged members, working through fronts such as "CyberAveng3rs".
  • 10 Nov: France summoned Iran's ambassador. FM Barrot said "We rule nothing out". Paris will seek EU sanctions and arrest warrants.
  • ASPI notes similar incidents in the past week in Belgium, Luxembourg, Sweden, Finland, Ireland and Estonia. Who did those is less clear.
  • 9 Nov: At least 10 US KC-135 tankers left RAF Mildenhall and three Ospreys moved to RAF Lakenheath. Both bases went to FPCON Charlie, the second-highest alert. The Wall Street Journal says an Iranian threat prompted it; US officials have not described the threat. It follows the removal of US bombers from RAF Fairford in September.
  • UK PM Burnham says the bases are safe. MI5 says Iran-linked operations led to more than 50 arrests in a year.
  • Iran denies links to specific attacks in Britain, but the IRGC calls bases used by US forces legitimate targets.
Why it matters for Australia

Australia hosts US forces and joint facilities (Pine Gap, Darwin, Tindal, HMAS Stirling), so the UK experience is a preview of what hosting can cost in a US war. The fridge hacks hit everyday internet-connected equipment, not hardened networks. ASPI's point is that Australia's food, fuel, energy and port systems are linked in the same way: "The attacks have not hit Australia, but the country is not immune." ASD already advises operators to be able to isolate critical systems. Australia has also listed the IRGC as a state sponsor of terrorism, so it is not a neutral in Tehran's eyes.

State media framing · Tehran and Moscow

Tehran denies and counter-threatens. The Foreign Ministry rejected France's "baseless" claim and warned it would take measures to protect its interests. Earlier in the week Iranian outlets simply did not mention the French charge. Press TV reported the UK aircraft move as due to an "alleged" Iranian threat. RT mocked Paris for blaming Iran a week after pinning the same hacks on Russia. Xinhua flashed the aircraft evacuation as a media report, without comment. No statement on Australia was found.

Angle to build

Deterrence by reach: Iran cannot hit the US homeland easily, so it raises costs for the allies that host US forces, using deniable cyber units and hired criminals. How should a host nation like Australia price that risk into decisions about US basing and access?

Taiwan says the Liaoning group is running 'area denial' drills as Beijing courts Washington before APEC

What happened
  • 10 Nov: Taiwan's defence minister Wellington Koo said China "has indeed conducted area denial drills in the Western Pacific", involving the Liaoning carrier group. China has declared no drills and its defence ministry did not comment. Last week Taiwan counted more than 100 Chinese navy and coast guard ships from the East China Sea to the Pacific.
  • At the same time Beijing is sending calm signals to Washington. On 9 Nov it extended its pause on measures against 11 US firms for two months. On 10 Nov the PLA handed over remains of US WWII airmen in Chengdu.
  • Trump and Xi are due to meet at APEC in Shenzhen on 18–19 Nov. SCMP expects few deliverables, with bigger items left for the G20 in Miami in December. Meduza, citing the WSJ, says the US, China and Russia cannot agree on a format for a three-way leaders' meeting.
  • The Nvidia affair hangs over it. The ABC reports CEO Jensen Huang had an affair with an alleged Chinese intelligence asset. Congress is pressing for tighter chip curbs. RT reports Trump is backing Huang despite an FBI probe.
  • Pressure on US allies continues: Global Times ran pieces against Japan and the Philippines, and China's coast guard patrolled around the Senkakus on 9 Nov.
  • Next week: ASEAN summit in Manila (16–18 Nov). Lowy says Washington has signalled a Quad leaders' meeting on the sidelines, the first in more than two years.
Why it matters for Australia

Area denial drills east of Taiwan rehearse keeping US and allied forces, which could include Australian ones, out of a Taiwan fight. That is the scenario AUKUS submarines and northern base upgrades are meant for. The pattern also matters: Beijing is easing off Washington while leaning on Tokyo and Manila, Australia's two closest regional defence partners. A Trump–Xi meeting with a friendly tone and little content could leave allies carrying the pressure. The Quad meeting in Manila is where Albanese can test how much the US still backs the grouping.

State media framing · Beijing

Chinese state media did not report the drills Taiwan described; last month Beijing called the Liaoning's training annual and routine. The US line is warm: Xinhua said the airmen handover should add "positive factors" to "constructive strategic stability" (translated from Chinese), and Global Times urged turning differences into cooperation. On Nvidia, Global Times called it a "personal controversy" and the Congressional push "McCarthyist" and a "witch hunt". The allies get the opposite treatment. Global Times said Tokyo has "a guilty conscience", called Manila a "testing ground" for Japan to break postwar limits, and told the Philippines it needs "statecraft, not stagecraft". Nothing in this batch on Australia, AUKUS or the Quad.

Angle to build

Beijing is running a wedge strategy: stability for Washington, pressure for Washington's allies. Does a transactional Trump–Xi relationship make US allies safer (less risk of war) or more exposed (less US backing in grey-zone disputes)?

PNG pays for dropping Taiwan as Australia opens ADF recruiting there; AUKUS critics and defenders trade blows

What happened
  • Taiwan confirmed it will close its Port Moresby office (open since 1990), pull all staff and end all aid and technical programs. PNG ordered the mission shut in July, citing its One-China policy. Beijing welcomed that.
  • Taiwan has also stopped buying PNG LNG and will sign no new spot contracts. RNZ says Taiwan took roughly a third of PNG's LNG exports. Taipei says the move will have "a substantial impact" on PNG's export earnings, and blames Chinese "political pressure and economic influence". RNZ gives no independent evidence of that pressure.
  • Applications open on 16 Nov for 100 PNG students and graduates aged 18–25 to join the ADF, under the 2025 Pukpuk Treaty. The pilot costs A$65m and the first cohort is due to be sworn in by late 2027. An ASPI piece warns that with thousands likely to be rejected, selection must be openly scored or it "will look corrupt".
  • AUKUS: the privately run AUKUS Public Inquiry (chaired by former ADF chief Chris Barrie and Peter Garrett) reported on 4 Nov, calling AUKUS "the worst, riskiest and most costly defence decision ever". On 10 Nov a US Studies Centre fellow replied in ASPI's Strategist that the verdict "lacks credibility" because it plays down China's coercion, while accepting that delivery timelines and nuclear waste are real concerns.
  • Also: a no-confidence motion against Vanuatu PM Jotham Napat was withdrawn on 9 Nov.
Why it matters for Australia

PNG is Australia's nearest neighbour and now a treaty ally. Losing a buyer for a third of its LNG hurts Port Moresby's budget, and Canberra is its lender of last resort. If Chinese buyers or finance fill the gap, Beijing's leverage grows in the one Pacific state Australia has bound itself to defend. The ADF recruitment pilot is a test of whether the Pukpuk Treaty delivers something ordinary Papua New Guineans can see and trust. The AUKUS exchange shows the home debate hardening into two camps that disagree on the basic question: how big is the China threat?

State media framing · Beijing

Quiet. Beijing welcomed PNG's July decision, but the digests found no Chinese state media comment on Taiwan's exit or the LNG halt, and nothing on the ADF recruitment or the AUKUS inquiry. The only Australia item in Chinese state media was Global Times on the fatal stabbing of a Chinese delivery rider in Melbourne, which it said "sparked safety concerns" in the Chinese community. The silence on AUKUS is worth noting: the inquiry's findings match long-standing Chinese talking points, yet Beijing has not picked them up so far.

Angle to build

Taiwan's exit shows what switching sides costs a small state, and who gets asked to cover the bill. Is Australia now underwriting the economic price of PNG's One-China choice? And why might Beijing stay silent on an Australian report that makes its case for it?

North Korea tests a 'high-speed torpedo' for 2027 service; Kim Yo Jong rebuffs the UN chief

What happened
  • 9 Nov: North Korea tested what KCNA calls a "new-type high-speed torpedo weapon system", fired in the East Sea (Sea of Japan). KCNA reported it on 10 Nov and said the navy will be equipped with it from the start of 2027.
  • It is the second public test in a month. After the first, on 15 Oct, KCNA claimed AI guidance, and some analysts suggested a supercavitating design. This time KCNA gave fewer details and no photos. The claims are not independently verified.
  • It follows a mid-October test of two types of submarine-launched cruise missile.
  • 10 Nov: Kim Yo Jong rejected UN Secretary-General Guterres's call for the two Koreas to reopen military hotlines after a mine blast in the DMZ. She said he would bear responsibility for any incident caused by the South.
  • Summit watch: Yonhap and NK News report experts saying a Democratic House cannot stop a Trump–Kim summit but could limit what Trump offers. Pyongyang's apparent demand to be recognised as a nuclear power remains the obstacle.
Why it matters for Australia

A fast torpedo, sub-launched cruise missiles and a new destroyer class all point the same way: North Korea is building a navy that can threaten ships, not just a missile force. Australian ships and aircraft regularly help enforce UN sanctions on North Korea from Japan, in those waters. Australia is also a member of UN Command, so a border incident with hotlines cut is a direct interest. Any Trump–Kim deal that accepts North Korea's nuclear status would weaken the non-proliferation rules Australia relies on.

State media framing · Pyongyang, amplified by Tehran

KCNA kept it short: the "new-type torpedo weapon system is to be equipped by the Navy" from early 2027. Kim Yo Jong accused Guterres of a "prejudiced and irresponsible attitude" and warned against "reckless military provocations of the ROK gangsters". On 8 Nov Pyongyang called Seoul a "puppet" for joining a US maritime exercise. The same days' domestic coverage had Kim Jong Un on farming and rural life. Press TV twice promoted the torpedo test as Pyongyang "arming its navy", a small example of one sanctioned state boosting another. No North Korean statement on Australia was found.

Angle to build

Pyongyang announces a deployment date but hides the weapon. Is this a real capability or a bargaining chip before a possible Trump–Kim summit? Compare how much detail KCNA gives when it wants to deter versus when it wants to negotiate.
