Iran threatens US bases in Europe, claims two more mined tankers; Bessent moves to a military base

What happened
  • 11 Nov: Mohsen Rezaei, secretary of Iran's Supreme National Security Council, told Al Mayadeen that Iran "may target certain US military bases in Europe" if it is attacked again (as reported by Mehr). He said targets could include distant US installations and "certain islands", especially any used by aircraft that strike Iran. Iran would not limit itself to targets hit in earlier rounds.
  • Rezaei repeated that there are no direct talks with the US. Iran has put seven conditions and is waiting for a clear answer. He said no enriched nuclear material, not even a small amount, will leave Iran.
  • 11 Nov: The IRGC Navy said two more tankers hit mines while leaving the strait by an "unauthorized" route, with transponders off and under US air cover. It says both burned and were abandoned. No ship names or flags were given. This is unconfirmed. Maritime authorities have not verified the IRGC's recent mine-strike claims, and CENTCOM has said earlier in the war that no ships have hit mines in the strait.
  • Iran's strait authority said it took "legal action" against two bulk carriers using forged documents. Speaker Ghalibaf, via TASS, said Iran has an understanding with Oman on a shipping corridor and has had no official US reply to its conditions.
  • 10–11 Nov: The US Treasury confirmed that Secretary Scott Bessent and his family moved from their Washington home to a military base after Iranian threats (first reported by the New York Times). Bessent runs the sanctions campaign against Iran. Reuters saw a late-October intelligence memo warning that Iranian plots against Americans had reached an advanced stage. Iran denies plotting against US officials.
  • Oil: one market report on 11 Nov had Brent up about 1.4% to roughly US$92.77. I could not confirm this against a second source.
Why it matters for Australia

Two things matter here. First, fuel: every unverified mine claim still moves the oil price, and Australia buys most of its refined fuel from Asian refiners that run on Gulf crude. Second, hosting risk. Rezaei's logic is that a base becomes a target if US aircraft use it against Iran. He named Europe, not Australia. But Australia hosts US bombers, tankers and joint facilities at Tindal, Darwin and Pine Gap, and has listed the IRGC as a state sponsor of terrorism. The Bessent move shows Washington takes Iran's reach seriously enough to relocate a cabinet member.

State media framing · Tehran, with Moscow and Beijing relaying

Tehran presents the strait as a legal regime it enforces. The tankers are "violating" ships on an "unauthorized route", and the IRGC says mines stopped a US attempt to "smuggle the vessels" out. It says the incident adds to the "black record" of CENTCOM chief Brad Cooper. The Defence Ministry spokesman said the enemy must accept Iran's demands or "surrender on the battlefield" (IRNA headline). On Bessent, Tehran Times asks whether the West has "launched a new smear campaign" and says France accused Iran without "substantiated evidence". A Tehran Times commentary says Iran is rethinking "the nuclear threshold". TASS amplifies Ghalibaf's line that the strait's status will not go back to what it was. RT says Bessent "hides". Xinhua relays the New York Times account without comment and puts the IRGC's wording in quotation marks. None mentioned Australia.

Angle to build

Iran is building a deterrence-by-reach doctrine: any territory that helps a US strike becomes a target. How far does that logic travel? Compare Rezaei's criteria (bases used by attacking aircraft) with what US forces actually do from Australian soil.

Abu Dhabi ends: prisoner swap agreed, nothing on territory; US envoys go to Putin today

What happened
  • 11 Nov: Two days of Russia–Ukraine–US talks in Abu Dhabi ended. The sides agreed in principle to a large prisoner exchange. No numbers yet. Ukraine's Davyd Arakhamia said it could be "possibly even the largest overall". Russia's Vladimir Medinsky said details would come soon.
  • Territory did not move. Jared Kushner called it "the hardest" issue. Medinsky said "every inch of our land will be returned to Russia".
  • Security guarantees: Kushner reported "significant progress" and Ukraine's Rustem Umerov "huge progress". Neither gave details.
  • European security: Medinsky called it a central topic, with agreement on some points only. He said his team did not deal with the Europeans in Abu Dhabi.
  • Mood: Medinsky said the sides had "moved closer to peace". Arakhamia spoke of "good momentum" for a deal by the New Year.
  • 12 Nov: Kushner and Steve Witkoff are due to meet Putin in Moscow this morning (Moscow time). Putin aide Yuri Ushakov: "they're bringing something". Zelenskyy says he will meet the two envoys in Dublin the same day. Ushakov also says a Putin–Trump meeting at APEC is being worked on.
  • Zelenskyy asked Europe to name one representative for the talks by the end of November or at a Berlin meeting on 19 Nov.
  • 11 Nov: The CSTO summit in Moscow stripped Armenia of its voting rights, officially over unpaid dues. Armenia froze its participation in 2024.
  • 11 Nov: Russia's ambassador to the US, Alexander Darchiev, died aged 66. No cause was given.
  • The war goes on: Russia claimed 627 Ukrainian drones downed in one night (10–11 Nov) and says it took Voskresenka in Zaporizhzhia region. Reuters, via Meduza, says Ukrainian strikes have hit plants holding about 30% of Russia's refining capacity. Russia's battlefield claims are not independently verified.
Why it matters for Australia

The shape of the deal is the precedent. Prisoners are the easy part; territory and the European security order are the hard parts, and Europe still has no seat. Australia has backed Ukraine with more than A$1.5b and sanctions on Russia, so a settlement by New Year would raise quick questions about sanctions relief. The Armenia move is a smaller lesson in how Moscow treats a partner that drifts west. And a Putin–Trump meeting at APEC would happen in the same room as Albanese.

State media framing · Moscow

Moscow frames the talks as going its way and on its terms. Medinsky, via TASS, said Russia will be satisfied "only when we win and sign a peace". Lavrov said the trilateral format "makes the most sense". RT called the talks a "major shift" because Washington will now discuss Moscow's European security ideas. Europe is kept out and mocked. Zakharova says EU and NATO representatives have no place at the talks. RT labels Zelenskyy's request for a European seat an "ultimatum" and says Europe has "effectively been sidelined". Medinsky said Europeans can join "when they grow a conscience". RT also says Merz is holding the EU budget "hostage" over Ukraine aid. Shoigu says the front has "changed significantly" since Anchorage. On Armenia, the Kremlin line is procedural: Peskov said Armenia "has failed to meet its obligations". Rybar adds posts on Yerevan's "British patronage". None mentioned Australia.

Angle to build

Both delegations sound upbeat while the core dispute (territory) is untouched. Is the optimism real, or is each side performing for Trump so the other gets blamed if talks fail? Watch what Witkoff and Kushner carry out of Moscow today.

Rubio and Wang clear the path to Trump–Xi in Shenzhen; Albanese heads to APEC and Beijing

What happened
  • 11 Nov: Secretary of State Rubio and Foreign Minister Wang Yi spoke by phone to prepare the Trump–Xi meeting at APEC in Shenzhen on 18–19 Nov. It would be their third face-to-face meeting this year. Xinhua quoted Wang calling the call "positive and constructive". There was no immediate US readout.
  • Rubio said on 10 Nov that work is under way on a three-way Trump–Xi–Putin meeting at APEC. Trump has said he would likely meet both to discuss ending the Iran war (per SCMP). SCMP also reports Iran pointing to a Chinese role in breaking its deadlock with Washington.
  • Australia: the PM's office said on 10 Nov that Albanese will go to COP31 in Antalya, then Manila for the ASEAN–Australia and East Asia summits, then APEC in Shenzhen, then Beijing for the Annual Leaders' Meeting with Premier Li. The release does not mention a Quad leaders' meeting in Manila, which Rubio has flagged.
  • Taiwan: Beijing still has not acknowledged the drills that Taiwan's defence minister described on 10 Nov as "area denial" training by the Liaoning group.
  • Japan: Xinhua and People's Daily are running hard on a report that a senior Japanese official wants nuclear weapons allowed into Japan in emergencies. I could not confirm the original remark independently. China's embassy also wants Tokyo to "severely punish" the Self-Defense Force officer who broke into it in March. Prosecutors have sought 18 months' jail; the verdict is due 24 Dec.
Why it matters for Australia

Albanese will be in Shenzhen while Trump and Xi meet, then sits down with Li in Beijing. That makes next week the main test of Australia's "stabilisation" policy this year. The risk is being squeezed: a warm Trump–Xi meeting lowers the cost to Beijing of pressing US allies, and Japan and the Philippines are already getting that treatment. If the Quad leaders do meet in Manila days before, Beijing will read Albanese's visit against it. A US–China–Russia conversation on Iran would also decide fuel-price questions for Australia in a room it is not in.

State media framing · Beijing

Two tones, by audience. To Washington, calm: Wang said the current stability "has not come easily" and is "a rational choice made by the two major countries". CGTN gave warm coverage to the handover of US WWII airmen's remains. To US allies and Taiwan, pressure. Global Times says experts dismiss the hype about "silent military drills" and that "Taiwan independence" provocations are the real risk. It marked the PLA Air Force's 77th anniversary by saying an "ever-stronger" force "contributes to peace". Japan is the main target, over the nuclear remark and the embassy break-in. On the Nvidia affair, Global Times sees "too many China-related 'coincidences'". CGTN used George Yeo to argue APEC exists "to keep the Pacific from being divided". Nothing in the digests on Australia, AUKUS or the Quad.

Angle to build

Beijing has said nothing yet about Albanese's visit or the Quad meeting. When it does, compare the tone with what it uses for Tokyo and Manila. Does Australia get the "Washington" treatment (stability) or the "ally" treatment (pressure), and what would move it from one to the other?

Where Australia puts its new air defences, and what the US midterms change for the alliance

What happened
  • 11 Nov: ASPI's John Coyne argued that Australia's new integrated air and missile defences should mostly be based in the north. The government committed a further A$2.4b to Air 6500 (the joint air battle management system) in September. The Army's NASAMS short-range system became fully operational that month. A A$299.1m works proposal at RAAF Darwin would support the deployable air control unit.
  • Coyne's test is time: basing should follow how fast protection must be in place and how long it must last. He wants exercises that measure how long it takes to authorise, move, set up and sustain air defence units at Darwin, Tindal, Townsville and HMAS Stirling. Networks, he says, do not replace having interceptors where they are needed.
  • 11 Nov: Breaking Defense reported experts backing the Army's shift to littoral (coastal and island) operations. The Littoral Manoeuvre Group was set up in Brisbane in March. It will have three landing craft battalions, each with four medium and two heavy craft built by Austal. ANU's John Blaxland called it "a radical organisational design concept" but warned funding could slow it, with a budget review under way to pay for AUKUS submarines.
  • 11 Nov: ASPI's Fitriani and Justin Bassi argued the US midterms show "political alignments can change quickly but national interests endure". Democrats won both chambers on 3 Nov. They urge Canberra to lock cooperation into institutions: the Quad, the Australia–US–Japan–Philippines group, and an upgraded partnership with Manila.
  • 10 Nov: The US House voted 211–204 to send a resolution to impeach Defence Secretary Hegseth to committee. Republican Thomas Massie's eight articles include War Powers breaches over Iran and a US strike on an Iranian primary school. Breaking Defense says it is unlikely to advance before the new Congress sits in 2027.
Why it matters for Australia

These are the practical questions behind the strategy documents. Northern bases host US bombers, Marines and, from 2027, more submarine visits at Stirling. Iran's new threat to US bases in Europe is a reminder that hosting without air defence is a gamble. The littoral force is how the Army gets to the islands to Australia's north. Both compete with AUKUS for money. In Washington, a Democratic Congress from January and a wounded Pentagon chief mean more oversight and more uncertainty about US defence budgets, which AUKUS submarine production depends on.

State media framing · Beijing, Moscow and Tehran (on US politics)

No state media comment on Australia's air defence or littoral plans was found. All three covered the Hegseth vote as proof of US disorder. Xinhua reported the House shelving the bid and CGTN ran it prominently. RT headlined that the House "blocks" the impeachment bid and called the midterms a "bloodbath". Tehran links it to the war: Tehran Times tied Massie's move to the "Minab school massacre", and IRNA ran Schumer calling the war a major "fiasco". Pezeshkian claimed US and Israeli "war crimes" backfired at the polls. The shared message is that the war on Iran is breaking American politics.

Angle to build

Coyne measures air defence in hours: how long to get a battery where it is needed. Apply that to the alliance too. If US political attention can swing in one election, what should Australia be able to do alone in the first days of a crisis in the north?

Taiwan starts pulling its people out of PNG; US seabed mining auction nears off American Samoa

What happened
  • 11 Nov: Taiwan's foreign ministry said it has begun withdrawing all diplomatic and technical staff from Papua New Guinea. A few will stay to wind up the office, which opened in 1990. PNG gave Representative Chiu Chen-yu and one other diplomat seven days to leave, and allowed one officer to stay up to a year to finish the closure.
  • About 100 Taiwanese live in PNG. Taiwan's missions in nearby countries will handle consular help.
  • Background: PNG ordered the office shut in July, citing its One-China policy. Taiwan confirmed the closure on 6 Nov, ended aid programs and stopped buying PNG LNG. Taiwan had taken about a third of PNG's LNG exports.
  • 19 Nov: The US plans to auction 31.5 million acres of deep-sea mining leases off American Samoa. A Devpolicy piece on 11 Nov warns of the risks for Pacific neighbours.
  • About 150 senior law enforcement officials met in Nouméa earlier this month on drug smuggling across the Pacific by sea, which RNZ quotes as "no longer a remote threat".
  • The Diplomat says Honiara has become a "staging ground for geopolitical competition" with little change for ordinary Solomon Islanders.
Why it matters for Australia

PNG is Australia's nearest neighbour and a treaty ally under the Pukpuk Treaty. The Taiwan exit is now physical, and the LNG loss is a real hole in Port Moresby's budget. Who fills it (Chinese buyers, or Australian support) will shape influence there. Applications for PNG citizens to join the ADF open on 16 Nov, so Canberra's offer is on show the same week. The US seabed auction puts Australia in an awkward spot: its main ally is pushing ahead with mining that many Pacific governments oppose, and Australia has just campaigned on its COP31 Pacific partnership.

State media framing · Beijing

Still quiet. The digests found no Chinese state media item on Taiwan's withdrawal from PNG, the LNG halt or the US seabed auction, and nothing on the Pacific generally. Beijing welcomed PNG's closure order in July. The nearest Taiwan line this period is Xinhua reporting the mainland "slamming" the DPP for hindering cross-Strait exchanges, and Global Times promoting the Xiamen–Kinmen bridge link. Beijing may see no need to comment on a win. The US auction is an easy target for a "resource grab" line; watch for whether Chinese outlets pick it up before 19 Nov.

Angle to build

Taiwan's Pacific footprint keeps shrinking, in formal allies and now in informal offices too. What does Australia lose, if anything, when Taiwan leaves a Pacific capital, given Canberra also holds a one-China policy?

North Korea publishes its rewritten constitution; Kim pushes drones and military AI

What happened
  • 10 Nov: North Korea's Naenara website posted the full text of the constitution as amended on 23 March. NK News says it confirms earlier reports: the country's territory is redefined and Kim Jong Un's powers are expanded. It closely matches a version leaked earlier by a Seoul National University professor. I could not read the exact wording, so treat the detail as second-hand.
  • It is the first time Pyongyang has shown the text since it pledged in early 2024 to rewrite the constitution, after Kim dropped peaceful unification as a goal.
  • 11 Nov: Kim Jong Un urged drone dominance and wider military use of AI, according to Indian broadcaster DD News. I could not check this against KCNA or Yonhap.
  • Also: Pyongyang has put its embassy building in Mexico up for sale for over US$2m (NK News). South Korea's foreign minister discussed North Korea with US and Ukrainian counterparts at the G7 meeting in Paris.
Why it matters for Australia

A constitution that defines territory and no longer treats the South as part of one nation turns border incidents into state-to-state disputes. That raises the stakes of events like the recent DMZ mine blast. Australia is a member of UN Command and helps enforce sanctions from Japan. The drone and AI push fits what North Korea is learning from Russia's war. Those lessons travel to other militaries in Australia's region. The embassy sale is a small sign of how short of cash the regime's diplomatic network is, and fits NK News reporting on its alleged cryptocurrency theft.

State media framing · Pyongyang

Pyongyang let the document speak for itself: it posted the text on an outward-facing site without fanfare, months after adopting it. That is the message. The two-state line is now law, not rhetoric. It fits Kim Yo Jong's statement this week blaming Seoul for border tension and rejecting the UN chief's call for dialogue with a charge of a "prejudiced and irresponsible attitude". I found no direct KCNA quotes on the constitution or the drone remarks in today's material. No North Korean statement on Australia was found.

Angle to build

Why publish now, eight months after the vote? Possible audiences: Seoul (after the mine blast), Washington (before any Trump–Kim summit), or the UN. Which reading fits the timing best?
