Qatar takes Iran's two-day Hormuz plan to Trump; no breakthrough yet

What happened
  • 9 Nov (US time): Trump met Qatari PM Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman Al Thani at the White House for just under an hour, behind closed doors. There was no public readout. Axios reported Qatar wanted Trump to give diplomacy more time.
  • The proposal Qatar carried, as reported: Iran reopens Hormuz and stops attacks on Gulf tankers. In return the US lifts its naval and economic blockade and releases about US$8b of Iranian funds held in Qatar. Iran has cut its proposed implementation period from seven days to two, which spokesman Baqaei called a "sign of goodwill".
  • Trump on 60 Minutes, asked about escalating: "I'm not looking to. I don't think there's any reason to." He said Task Force Iron Gate, which extends the blockade to land routes, "is in full force and effect". Rubio said the sides are not "at the verge of a breakthrough" and called Iran's demands "unacceptable".
  • Iran's terms: Baqaei says there is "no reason" to send its 60% enriched uranium abroad. He says any deal must include lifting sanctions, ending the blockade, unfreezing assets and a ceasefire on all fronts. Iranian media cite a security source saying there will be no talks until the US acts on seven conditions (unconfirmed).
  • At sea: the UK's maritime agency (UKMTO) reclassified Saturday's tanker fire north of Khasab, Oman, as an attack. Iranian state TV said four tankers were "brought to a standstill" in 24 hours. Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline was hit again, causing a "limited fire" in Medina region.
  • Brent fell about 4.7% on 9 Nov to roughly US$93.75 (session low US$92.80). It had already dropped 16% the week before.
Why it matters for Australia

Australia imports most of its liquid fuel as refined product from Asian refineries that run on Gulf crude. A deal that reopens Hormuz would be the single biggest relief for petrol and diesel prices and for the RBA's inflation outlook. Equally, a breakdown would push prices back toward US$100. The Quad foreign ministers (31 Oct) listed Hormuz freedom of navigation as a shared priority, so Canberra is on record. Any mine-clearance or escort coalition after a deal could bring requests for ADF contributions.

State media framing · Tehran and Beijing

Tehran puts the onus on Washington. Araghchi says it is now the White House's turn to choose: accept the two-day plan or "choose war" (IRNA). The IRGC calls Trump's claims of controlling Hormuz and clearing mines "sheer lies" (Press TV). Tehran presents its own shipping stoppages as halting "smuggling". Beijing is more measured. Xinhua's Chinese-language feed reports both sides with careful attribution. It quotes Trump saying more strikes are "extremely unlikely" and the IRGC calling Iron Gate "just wordplay". China's Middle East envoy Zhai Jun met Iranian security officials in Tehran and called for "political solutions". Press TV goes further and presents China as rejecting "US-Israel aggression", which Beijing's own wording does not say.

Angle to build

Is Qatar's plan an interim ceasefire for shipping that puts off the nuclear question? If so, whom does that suit: Trump after the midterms, Tehran, or Beijing ahead of APEC? And what does it mean for Australia if Hormuz reopens while Iran's enriched uranium stockpile stays unresolved?

Russia, Ukraine and US resume peace talks in Abu Dhabi

What happened
  • 10–11 Nov: US-mediated trilateral talks in Abu Dhabi, the first since the process stalled when the Iran war began in late February.
  • Delegations: Witkoff and Kushner for the US. Ukraine sends Rustem Umerov with Kyrylo Budanov, Sergiy Kyslytsya and Davyd Arakhamia. Russia's reinforced team is led by Vladimir Medinsky and reportedly includes Sergei Ryabkov and Mikhail Galuzin.
  • Agenda: post-war security arrangements, de-escalation and humanitarian issues. Kushner says a "comprehensive and lasting settlement" needs Russia and Europe to deal directly on security.
  • An EU team led by Bjoern Seibert (von der Leyen's chief of staff) is attending, with E3 national security advisers, Italy and a NATO representative. Russia has not said whether it will meet them.
  • Both sides kept striking energy targets on the eve of the talks. Zelensky says Russia used North Korean-made KN-23 missiles on a key energy facility on Saturday (preliminary findings, site unnamed).
  • Trump has said Ukraine may end up taking "a little bit different shape" (via RT, unconfirmed wording).
Why it matters for Australia

Australia's support package for Ukraine and its sanctions on Russia are tied to how this settlement handles territory. A deal that rewards seizing land by force cuts against the rules-based order Canberra argues for in the Indo-Pacific. There is also a direct consular angle. ABC reports Daryl Fladung, 38, a Queensland security guard with no military experience, flew to Moscow in April and signed a Russian army contract. He has been missing since late June and is feared dead. He is the first Australian known to have joined Russia's army since 2022. DFAT would not confirm what it knows.

State media framing · Moscow

Moscow is setting terms and blaming Europe in advance. TASS reports Nebenzya saying Russia will reject a freeze that "preserves the existing security threat" (translated). Ryabkov claims the West plans to blockade Kaliningrad, which "would lead directly to disaster". Shoigu accuses Europe of "offensive military exercises", and the SVR says the EU wants "cannon fodder". TASS gives prominence to Kushner's call for Russia–Europe security talks and contrasts it with EU resistance. RT links Trump's "different shape" remark to Moscow's demand that "new territorial realities" be recognised. MoD Russia keeps reporting gains, most recently Vasilkovoye in Zaporozhye.

Angle to build

Moscow wants a US-brokered deal but casts Europe as the spoiler. Does putting the EU in the room strengthen Kyiv, or give Moscow a reason to stall? Compare this with how Beijing might try to split Washington from its Indo-Pacific allies in a future Taiwan negotiation.

Iran announces offensive doctrine with 'no geographical limits'

What happened
  • 9 Nov: Army spokesman Brig. Gen. Mohammad Akraminia said Iran's military doctrine has moved from a defensive to an offensive approach. "Effective response" without geographical limits is now operational strategy (ISNA, via ANI). IRNA ties this to new directives from Commander-in-Chief Mojtaba Khamenei.
  • The same day, President Pezeshkian said the Supreme Leader had ordered officials to end the state of "neither war nor peace". He added: "We favor dialogue and negotiations... we will not surrender to force and bullying."
  • Press TV ran an opinion piece listing possible targets: US bases across the Gulf, Jordan and Djibouti, plus Diego Garcia, undersea internet cables, energy terminals, Gulf desalination plants and Israeli nuclear and tech sites.
  • No source formally links the doctrine announcement to Pezeshkian's remarks. The link is implied by timing.
Why it matters for Australia

Naming Diego Garcia, and asserting that any territory that "facilitates attacks" on Iran is a target, is deterrence messaging aimed at US partners. Australia hosts US force posture and has ADF personnel in the Middle East. The threat to subsea cables on Asia–Europe routes matters to Australia's data links. Threats to Gulf energy and desalination plants feed straight into fuel prices.

State media framing · Tehran

Tehran presents the shift as defensive deterrence, not escalation. Press TV says Iran "is not racing to catch up" but lifting limits on what it already has. It describes Tehran as "drawing the map of the next potential war in advance". It also warns: "that territory is no longer off-limits to Iranian retaliation." Speaker Ghalibaf adds a wedge aimed at US domestic politics, saying Israel wants to drag the US into an "endless war". Beijing and Moscow have not commented on the doctrine in the outlets reviewed.

Angle to build

Is this a real change in Iranian military planning or bargaining leverage timed to the Qatar talks? How should countries hosting US facilities, including Australia, judge their exposure to a declared 'facilitator' doctrine?

Trump heads for APEC in Shenzhen and floats a Trump–Xi–Putin meeting

What happened
  • Trump says he expects to meet Putin and Xi together during his trip to the APEC leaders' meeting in Shenzhen on 18–19 Nov: "one of the meetings I think will be with President Putin and President Xi". He says the agenda includes "denuclearization" and ending the Iran war. Peskov says "work is underway". Beijing has announced no trilateral.
  • A Manila stop on 16 Nov has been reported (New York Post, citing the Philippine palace). This is not confirmed by the White House.
  • SCMP reports US military transport aircraft landing in Shenzhen, widely read as preparation for the visit.
  • 10 Nov: China's Commerce Ministry extended for two more months its suspension of export-control measures against 16 US entities (listed April 2025). It said this implements the consensus from the "eighth round" of US–China trade talks. The broader trade truce runs to January.
  • This would be the third Trump–Xi meeting this year, after Beijing in May and Washington in September.
Why it matters for Australia

A great-power deal over Iran or Ukraine struck in China, without allies present, is the 'Yalta 2.0' scenario Lowy's Interpreter warns about. Canberra would want assurance that Taiwan, the South China Sea and the alliance are not part of any trade-off. A stable US–China trade truce helps Australian exporters. A summit that reads as Washington accommodating Beijing would sharpen the domestic AUKUS debate. ASPI this week stressed that the PLA Southern Theater Command, not global Chinese dominance, is Australia's most direct military threat.

State media framing · Beijing and Moscow

Beijing is staging APEC as proof of its openness to trade. CGTN uses the import expo to promote APEC and Global South links, and Xinhua reports more than US$650m in US farm deals signed there. On the US, Xinhua's Chinese feed highlights division: Jeffries says Hegseth "must go", and Democrats plan to roll back "unreasonable" tariffs. On the Nvidia affair, FM spokesman Guo Jiakun called "Chinese spy" claims "pure speculation and slander" (translated). Beijing has not confirmed the trilateral. Moscow, through Peskov, calls it "extremely useful". Lowy argues it has little upside for Beijing with Europe.

Angle to build

Beijing hosting both Trump and Putin lets it play convenor on Iran and Ukraine. What would it want in return, and on which Indo-Pacific issues? How would Canberra detect and respond to a quiet trade-off?

Chinese consulate presses Victoria over killing of delivery rider in Melbourne

What happened
  • 6 Nov, about 8.10pm: a Chinese national working as a HungryPanda food-delivery rider was killed near Queen Victoria Market, Melbourne. Another rider was injured. Police say two riders confronted a man suspected of tampering with their bikes, and an altercation followed. A 51-year-old man was arrested.
  • The Chinese Consulate-General activated its "emergency consular protection mechanism". Consul-General Fang Xinwen phoned the Victorian Premier and asked that the perpetrator be "severely punished". Global Times reports the Premier promised justice.
  • A Weibo hashtag about the victim's long working hours drew more than 4.9 million views. SCMP reports online outrage in China over delivery-rider safety.
Why it matters for Australia

The case is now a bilateral consular issue as well as a criminal one. Beijing's public pressure on a state premier tests how Canberra and Victoria handle Chinese official advocacy while police and courts work independently. It also shapes perceptions of Australia among Chinese students, workers and tourists, a key relationship since stabilisation. Watch whether Beijing's framing stays on this one case or widens into claims about the safety of Chinese nationals in Australia.

State media framing · Beijing

So far the framing is restrained. Global Times reports the consulate calling police to "bring the perpetrator to justice" and to handle the case fairly. It makes no wider claims about Australia's safety. Xinhua's Chinese feed reports the consulate asking police to "severely punish the perpetrator" and protect Chinese citizens' rights (translated). State outlets give prominence to human-interest detail about the victim's long hours and his family back home. There is no commentary attacking Australia.

Angle to build

Compare this restrained tone with how Beijing framed incidents involving Chinese nationals during the 2020–22 downturn. What does that suggest about how much Beijing values the current stabilisation?
