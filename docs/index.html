Iran claims four more tankers hit in a day; top clerical body calls NPT exit an "unavoidable necessity"

What happened
  • Night of 11–12 Nov: The IRGC Navy said two "violating" supertankers carrying more than 4 million barrels were "stopped by massive explosions" after taking an "unauthorized southern route" through the Strait of Hormuz. It said both were burning. It gave no names, flags or casualties, and did not say what caused the blasts (Mehr).
  • 12 Nov, afternoon: Iran's Army said two more tankers hit a naval mine and caught fire in a "prohibited zone" east of the strait, after switching off their radar (Mehr, IRNA).
  • All four claims are unconfirmed. I found no independent or US account for these incidents. CENTCOM has called earlier IRGC mine claims false. I could not find a reliable oil price for 12 Nov.
  • 12 Nov: The Assembly of Experts, the clerical body that chooses the supreme leader, urged officials to examine leaving the Non-Proliferation Treaty. It called exit "an unavoidable necessity and a public and national demand" and asked for a review of defence doctrine to build deterrence. It warned of an immediate response "without geographic limits" to any new attack.
  • Iran International notes the Assembly has no direct say over nuclear policy, but is the first governing body to call for a doctrine review. It says this could open the way to reversing the late Ali Khamenei's religious ban on nuclear weapons. The Foreign Ministry said earlier this week that Iran remains committed to the NPT.
  • Armed forces spokesman Shekarchi said any US attack would get a response "ten times" stronger, after Trump reportedly threatened strikes five times heavier than before (Mehr; I could not confirm Trump's wording). Spokesman Baghaei warned third countries that no agreement with the US gives "legitimate grounds" to join an attack.
  • Cost in the Pacific: briefings to NZ Foreign Minister Peters, released under official information law, put extra fuel costs for Pacific countries at NZ$935 million from late February to the end of July. Tonga's fuel prices are at a record. PNG diesel is at its highest since April after a subsidy ended. The ADB used an emergency grant fund for the first time, for Tonga, Tuvalu and Kiribati.
Why it matters for Australia

Fuel first. Four claimed hits in a day, true or not, raise insurance and freight costs on the route that feeds the Asian refineries Australia buys from. The RNZ figures show where the bill lands: on Pacific budgets that Australia and New Zealand are already propping up, with Fiji acting as the fuel hub for its neighbours. Second, the NPT. Australia has built its non-proliferation policy, and its defence of AUKUS naval reactors, on the treaty. An Iranian exit would be the first since North Korea's in 2003 and would weaken the rules Canberra relies on. Third, Baghaei's warning to "third countries" extends the logic Rezaei used on bases this week: helping a US strike makes you a party.

State media framing · Tehran, with Beijing relaying

Tehran frames the tanker hits as law enforcement, not attacks. The ships are "violating" or "offending", the route "illegal and unsafe", and the IRGC warns any ship that works with the "U.S. smuggling army" will "meet the same fate". The Army blames "the liar U.S. president" first, then owners who "trust his false claims". IRNA ran a legal defence of "defensive maritime measures". On the NPT, Press TV pinned the Assembly's statement and tied it to the failure of global nuclear safeguards. The line is that deterrence is a "public and national demand", not a regime choice. Not everyone at home agrees: Tehran Times relayed Kayhan saying that continuing the present stand-off "could bring adversaries closer to their goals". Global Times relayed the IRGC claim with "violating" in quotation marks, and Xinhua gave straight coverage to an IRGC missile and drone exhibition. None mentioned Australia.

Angle to build

The Assembly cannot order an NPT exit, so why speak now? Test two readings: a bargaining signal to Washington before APEC, or the regime preparing its own public for a doctrine change. What evidence would separate them?

Putin meets US envoys: more talks, Europeans invited, territory unchanged; Moscow talks up a meeting with Trump and Xi

What happened
  • 12 Nov: Putin met Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner in the Kremlin, a day after the Abu Dhabi talks. It was Witkoff's tenth meeting with Putin. There was no joint news conference.
  • Witkoff: "I think progress was made" and "We've set a new meeting" of the three-way format. He said European representatives will join the next round. No date or place was given. Asked if peace was closer: "I hope so."
  • Kremlin aide Yuri Ushakov called the talks substantive, constructive and frank. He said the US side backed Putin's proposal for a joint humanitarian mission to Kostiantynivka (Konstantinovka in Russian) in Donetsk region, and that Russia needs five days to clear mines there.
  • Prisoners: the large exchange agreed in principle in Abu Dhabi is still being finalised. Witkoff called it the "largest in history". No numbers yet.
  • Territory: no movement. Before the meeting Putin said lasting peace is impossible with "grey zones" and the only line is "the state border" (Meduza). The Moscow Times says he shows no sign of softening his demand for all of the Donbas.
  • Putin restated his security demands: no further NATO expansion and a return to NATO's 1997 force posture. Ushakov called European troops in Ukraine "a recipe for an endless war".
  • APEC: Ushakov said Putin and Trump will continue on Ukraine in Shenzhen, and that preparations are under way for a three-way meeting with Xi. Beijing has not confirmed one; reports differ on how settled it is.
  • Same day: Zelenskyy told European leaders in Dublin that Starshield, the military version of Starlink, has been throttled for days, so interceptor drones cannot catch Russian jet drones. "This helps Russians kill more Ukrainians." Kyiv is asking Washington why. The envoys were due in Dublin afterwards; I could not confirm that meeting took place.
  • Russia said it struck a drone plant in Kyiv, power substations in Zhytomyr and cargo ships bound for Odesa. A Russian colonel was killed by a car bomb in St Petersburg (Meduza). Russia's strike claims are not independently verified.
Why it matters for Australia

Two things to watch. One is the model: Washington and Moscow settle the outline, then Europe is invited in to a format it did not design. Australia is a US ally in a region where the same three leaders may meet next week. The other is APEC itself. If Trump, Xi and Putin meet in Shenzhen, Albanese will be in the building but not in the room, and the agenda (Ukraine, Iran, possibly arms control) touches Australian sanctions, fuel prices and the alliance. The Starshield problem is a practical lesson too: the ADF also depends on US commercial space services that someone else can turn down.

State media framing · Moscow

Moscow presents the meeting as Washington coming around to its terms. TASS says the envoys "strongly supported" Putin's humanitarian proposal and that Russia is ready to discuss European security "in tandem with the United States". The Konstantinovka mission, Ushakov said, would show who really controls the city. Europe is cast as the spoiler: Peskov says it is "talking about peace" while moving towards more war, and RT ran an analyst saying the EU cannot "speak with one voice". Kyiv is the other obstacle; Ushakov said Zelenskyy avoids peace because it would mean elections. TASS gave warm rolling coverage of the envoys' arrival while the Defence Ministry announced strikes the same afternoon. Dmitriev said it "matters a great deal" that European security is on the table. TASS also plays up the three-way summit with China far more than Chinese media do. No mention of Australia.

Angle to build

Moscow is using the talks to reopen the whole European security order, not just end a war. If Washington accepts that frame in Europe, what stops Beijing asking for the same conversation about Asia? Zakharova's call this week for "equal, indivisible" Asia-Pacific security is worth reading alongside.

Indonesia's vice president resigns; Prabowo accepts as a treason probe hits the Jokowi camp

What happened
  • 12 Nov: Vice President Gibran Rakabuming Raka, 39, handed his resignation to the People's Consultative Assembly (MPR). He did so hours before the lower house was due to consider an impeachment petition signed by more than 120 members.
  • President Prabowo accepted it and thanked Gibran for his "dedication, commitment and hard work".
  • Cause: his school credentials. The Constitutional Court found in October there was no compelling evidence he held the high school diploma needed to run. The Supreme Court then ruled his equivalency certificate invalid.
  • Gibran is the son of former president Joko Widodo (Jokowi). Jokowi's backing helped Prabowo win in 2024. The ABC says the resignation could end that alliance.
  • Next: the MPR must meet within 60 days. The president nominates two candidates and the MPR picks one. Names in play (ABC): Puan Maharani of PDI-P, former foreign minister Sugiono, and Gerindra's Sufmi Dasco Ahmad.
  • In parallel: former police chief Listyo Sigit Prabowo, a Jokowi ally, was detained last week with other senior officers over alleged wiretapping of the president. Xinhua reports police have opened a treason investigation into four officials, including a former presidential chief of staff. I have not confirmed the Xinhua detail elsewhere.
  • There are also protests in several cities over government policy and wildfires. Melbourne University's Vedi Hadiz says Prabowo is "on the defensive, if not on the retreat".
Why it matters for Australia

Indonesia is Australia's largest neighbour and a new treaty partner on security. A president fighting his predecessor's network, with the police leadership under a treason investigation, will be inward-looking and may lean more on the military. That matters for the police-to-police and defence links Australia depends on for counter-terrorism, people smuggling and maritime security. The replacement is a signal too: a PDI-P vice president would mean a broader coalition, a Gerindra loyalist would mean Prabowo is closing ranks. Prabowo is also due at the same summits as Albanese next week.

State media framing · Beijing

Beijing is reporting, not commenting. Xinhua's Telegram channel ran the treason investigation as straight news, naming the wiretapping case and the four officials (translated from Chinese). I found no Chinese, Russian or Iranian editorial line on Gibran's resignation and no statement from any of them. That silence is normal: Beijing treats leadership fights in ASEAN capitals as internal affairs and keeps its options open with whoever wins. Compare that with the Global Times piece this week calling Philippine defence chief Teodoro "an isolated figure in Manila". Beijing comments on domestic politics when the target is a US ally it wants to pressure.

Angle to build

Does a weaker Prabowo make Indonesia an easier or harder partner for Australia? Consider who gains inside Jakarta (the military, Gerindra, PDI-P) and what each wants from Canberra, Beijing and Washington.

Xi offers Taiwan talks with "people of vision", rules out any room for independence

What happened
  • 12 Nov: Xi Jinping spoke at the Great Hall of the People to mark 160 years since Sun Yat-sen's birth. About 3,000 people attended; Wang Huning chaired.
  • On Taiwan, Xi called reunification an "unwavering historical mission" of the Communist Party. He said Beijing is willing to hold "dialogue and consultations with people of vision and insight" in Taiwan, on the basis of the one-China principle and the 1992 Consensus, and would "give full consideration to Taiwan's realities".
  • The hard edge: Beijing "will never leave any room" for "Taiwan independence" activity "in any form". Reunification "must be realized" and "can, without doubt, be realized". Taipei Times notes he did not mention force.
  • 11 Nov: The Taiwan Affairs Office promised more measures for cross-strait economic integration.
  • Taipei's response: Mainland Affairs Council deputy minister Liang Wen-chieh said the wording "is not much different from the past". An unnamed Taiwanese official said Beijing's praise of Sun is "a mere facade" because the things Sun did, such as founding parties and newspapers, are crimes in China.
  • In Taipei the same day, former KMT figures presented a "cross-strait peace initiative" signed by more than 200 pan-blue academics.
  • Taiwan is sending Acer founder Stan Shih to APEC in Shenzhen and has asked for "equal footing" (SCMP, via the digests). SCMP also reports Taiwan will close its Papua New Guinea office later this month and halt spot-market LNG purchases; I could not open that article to check the detail.
Why it matters for Australia

This is Beijing setting its Taiwan line six days before Trump lands in Shenzhen. The offer of talks is aimed at the KMT and business, not the elected government, and at a US president who likes deals. If Trump softens US language on Taiwan to get a trade or Iran outcome, Australia's own position (support for the status quo, no unilateral change) gets harder to hold alongside Washington's. Albanese meets Premier Li in Beijing straight after APEC and should expect to be asked to repeat one-China language. The PNG office closure is the same campaign at work in Australia's nearest neighbour.

State media framing · Beijing, with Moscow echoing

Carrot and stick, run together. CGTN and Xinhua lead with Sun's legacy and "national rejuvenation", then the Taiwan passage. A Global Times editorial asks whether Taiwan "really understands" the event's signals (headline as given in the digest; I could not open the text). People's Daily reports the mainland attacking the DPP for hindering exchanges, and the Taiwan Affairs Office offers to share "modernization opportunities". TASS picked out the hardest line, that Xi ruled out any room for separatism. Towards Washington the tone stays warm: the Wang–Rubio call was "positive and constructive" and People's Daily is promoting "Kuliang friendship". Towards Japan it is the reverse, with a history campaign around the Tokyo Trials anniversary warning against attempts to "revive militarism". On Australia, People's Daily Online approvingly quoted the trade minister on a "constructive, stable relationship with China". Nothing on AUKUS or the Quad.

Angle to build

Who are the "people of vision"? Xi's phrasing invites Taiwanese parties and business to deal around their own government. Compare that with how Moscow now talks past Kyiv and Brussels to Washington. Is this a shared method for dealing with smaller democracies?

US admiral says AUKUS is "on plan"; four-nation hub for the Philippines; new counter-terrorism centre opens in Sydney

What happened
  • 12 Nov: Rear Admiral Chris Cavanaugh, who commands US Pacific Fleet submarines, told a Naval Submarine League symposium: "The most consequential allied initiative is AUKUS, which is on plan and in several areas ahead of schedule." He did not say which areas. Breaking Defense reports no figures on US submarine production, which is the usual worry.
  • His evidence was the reactivation of Submarine Squadron 3 this year to support Submarine Rotational Force-West at HMAS Stirling. USS Colorado arrived in Western Australia on 27 Oct for a month of maintenance. Cavanaugh: "They'll return at the end of next year and establish their first rotational force there."
  • Pillar II: the three navies have just finished the Maritime Big Play exercise off California. The Royal Navy called it their "largest ever demonstration of uncrewed and autonomous technology", with more than 40 technologies tested.
  • The "Squad" (Australia, Japan, the Philippines, the US): the defence ministers' joint statement from their 28 Oct meeting in Cebu, published by Defence on 10 Nov, lists a Combined Coordination Center in the Philippines. The Diplomat reported it this week. I could not open the statement itself, so the location and start date are unconfirmed.
  • 13 Nov: The A$76 million Counter Terrorism Online Centre opens in Sydney, joining ASIO and the AFP to find young people being radicalised online. Children are 40 per cent of this year's counter-terrorism caseload. Minister Tony Burke said the agencies are moving "from the meeting room to the chat room". A 17-year-old in NSW was charged with terrorism offences on 12 Nov.
  • Also: hackers stole personal data from the parks booking system used by Queensland, Victoria and South Australia (ABC, 12 Nov). APRA will stress-test banks and super funds against severe AI scenarios.
Why it matters for Australia

Cavanaugh's line is useful in Washington, where a new Democratic Congress will review AUKUS from January. But "on plan" covers the parts the US Navy controls: basing and rotations in Western Australia. It says nothing about whether US yards can build enough Virginia-class boats to sell three to Australia from 2032. The coordination centre is the Squad becoming a standing arrangement, with Australian officers likely working beside US, Japanese and Philippine staff near the South China Sea. That is a commitment Beijing will notice just as Albanese heads to Shenzhen and Beijing. The online centre reflects ASIO's view that the domestic threat is younger, faster and less tied to any one ideology.

State media framing · Beijing

No state media response to Cavanaugh's remarks or the coordination centre appeared in the digests or my searches. Beijing's standing line is that AUKUS drives "bloc confrontation", but it has not said so this week. Instead it is working on Manila: Global Times calls defence chief Teodoro, the Squad's host, "an isolated figure in Manila", and CGTN launched a soft documentary on civilian life on Ganquan Dao in the Paracels. Zakharova, via Xinhua, said Russia–China cooperation is key to Asia-Pacific stability and backed an "equal, indivisible" security architecture, a formula aimed at US alliances. On US technology politics, Global Times says the Nvidia founder's reported resignation over "Chinese spy" claims shows political interference in the tech sector. The quiet on Australia fits the week: Beijing wants a smooth Albanese visit.

Angle to build

Read the admiral's claim as a test of sources. What would you need to see to check "ahead of schedule"? List the AUKUS milestones that are public (Stirling works, rotation dates, Virginia build rate, Australian crew numbers) and who controls each.

Kim inspects a new east coast naval base; Pyongyang rejects the G7's denuclearisation call

What happened
  • 11 Nov (reported by KCNA on 12 Nov): Kim Jong Un inspected a "major naval base" under construction at Munchon, north of Wonsan on the east coast. NK News says work there began more than 12 years ago and restarted in August, and that it is the second big naval base project under way.
  • KCNA said Kim gave guidance on building "the center of the Korean-style naval command". Yonhap reports he urged it be finished on time and pledged advanced warships. Via Xinhua, he tied it to "maritime sovereignty".
  • The visit follows a test of a new high-speed torpedo reported on 10 Nov, said to be planned for deployment in 2027.
  • 12 Nov: Foreign Minister Choe Son-hui rejected the G7 foreign ministers' call for denuclearisation and said the nuclear course remains "steadfast" (Yonhap, via the digests). Pyongyang separately warned it would act against a US-led naval build-up. I could not open either Yonhap report, so I do not have the full wording.
  • Seoul's defence minister said the South keeps the conventional edge but the North is closing the technology gap. Seoul sees little chance of a Trump–Kim meeting around APEC. The unification minister called the North's shows of force a "kind of signal" on talks.
  • NK News also reports North Korea has changed its flag's proportions for the first time since 1948.
Why it matters for Australia

North Korea is building a navy to go with its missiles: new destroyers, torpedoes, and now a command base on the Sea of Japan side. Australian ships and aircraft patrol those waters from Japan to enforce UN sanctions, and Australia sits in UN Command. A more capable North Korean fleet makes those patrols riskier. The base also faces Russia's Pacific Fleet, and naval technology is one of the things Pyongyang is believed to be getting from Moscow in return for troops and shells.

State media framing · Pyongyang, with Beijing and Moscow relaying

KCNA presents the base as nation-building as much as war-fighting: a centre of "naval culture" as well as command, paired in the same report with Kim inspecting new housing and calling for an end to the urban-rural gap. The message at home is that a strong navy and better living standards go together. Abroad, Choe's statement restates that nuclear status is not up for negotiation, and that the G7 has no standing to ask. Xinhua and TASS both relayed the naval base visit without comment, which is their usual treatment and a form of cover. No North Korean statement on Australia was found.

Angle to build

Why a navy now? Consider three drivers: Russian technology on offer, the lesson from Iran that sea denial gives leverage, and US–Japan–South Korea naval exercises. Which best explains a command centre on the east coast?
