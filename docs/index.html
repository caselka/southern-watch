Beijing praises a "trusted partner" Australia while targeting Japan's nuclear submarine plans

What happened
  • Albanese met Xi in Shenzhen on Friday 20 November, then went to Beijing for the 11th Annual Leaders' Meeting with Premier Li Qiang. He also met NPC chairman Zhao Leji.
  • Xinhua quotes Xi saying the two countries should be "mutually trusted partners". He also called Albanese a bit of a "China hand".
  • CGTN reports Li promised a "favourable environment and stable expectations" for business and pushed cooperation on green development and AI.
  • On 21 November People's Daily published the full Joint Outcomes of the Annual Leaders' Meeting. A joint statement on climate change and the clean-energy transition came out the night before. The Australian government's version of the text was not checked for this brief.
  • Before the Beijing leg, the beef quota (filled in June, with 55% tariffs on extra shipments), iron ore pricing and Darwin Port were all unresolved. No source seen here says any of them was settled.
  • Also on 21 November, Xinhua cited a Kyodo report on Japan's draft security documents. It says they would launch a study of nuclear-powered submarines for FY2027–31 and allow nuclear weapons into Japan in an emergency. People's Daily reported tens of thousands of Japanese signing a petition against the submarines.
Why it matters for Australia

Canberra is getting what it wanted: stabilisation, trade, and no public fight over AUKUS while the PM is in China. The open trade irritants (beef, iron ore, Darwin Port) are where any thaw would be tested. The Japan submarine campaign matters more. Beijing is using a close Australian partner to try out arguments about non-proliferation and "nuclear submarines as a destabilising act", and these could later be pointed at AUKUS, especially if Japan ever moves closer to AUKUS Pillar II or submarine cooperation.

State media framing · Beijing

The coverage of Australia is entirely positive. Xinhua leads with Xi's "mutually trusted partners". CGTN headlines "China vows stable business ties with Australia" and has Zhao Leji marking the 55th anniversary of ties. People's Daily ran the joint outcomes as routine cooperation, with no attack lines on AUKUS, the Quad or the Manila Quad meeting. Japan gets the opposite treatment. The Foreign Ministry says Tokyo must "match its words with actions" on the four political documents. Global Times says Takaichi's pledge is no retraction of her "erroneous remarks". The submarine story is framed as public opposition and the "erosion" of Japan's non-nuclear principles.

Angle to build

Is Beijing playing a deliberate split-screen, warm to Canberra and cold to Tokyo, to separate the two closest US allies in the region? How long will the AUKUS silence last once Japan's submarine study becomes official?

Iran says Hormuz stays shut until its "seven conditions" are met; F-16 claim denied

What happened
  • Foreign Minister Araghchi says reopening the Strait of Hormuz is "irrelevant" until the US meets Iran's seven conditions, which now replace the Islamabad memorandum of understanding. He says a short plan for reopening is still on offer and Washington has not answered. Messages still pass through mediators.
  • Araghchi also says enriched uranium will stay under Iranian control and will not be shipped abroad. Trump told Fox he wants a "permanent" deal with no Iranian nuclear weapon, and says Iran's current terms are unacceptable.
  • Deputy FM Gharibabadi calls the US blockade "an act of war" and says Iran will break it. Deputy Speaker Haji Babaei warned that Gulf states backing the US "must bear the consequences".
  • Iran's Army says one of its missiles damaged a US F-16 over the Gulf and forced it to land. CENTCOM denies any F-16 was attacked (as reported by Xinhua and TASS).
  • The IRGC claims it shot down a 58th MQ-9 and a 19th MQ-1. It also says tankers on an "unauthorised" route caught fire after hitting mines near Khasab, Oman. UKMTO reported an attack on a ship. Iran's tallies are unverified.
  • The US Treasury sanctioned four Iranians who collect oil revenue, plus former IRGC intelligence official Mohammad Javad Bavand. Opposition outlet Iran International reports hardliners pushing for a tanker convoy to challenge the blockade.
Why it matters for Australia

Australia imports most of its refined fuel from Asian refineries that rely on Gulf crude. A longer closure of Hormuz and a mine campaign keep pressure on fuel prices and on Australia's thin reserve stocks. Iran's threats against Gulf states that host US forces raise the risk to Australian personnel and contractors in the region, and to any ADF contribution to maritime security. Iran refusing to give up its enriched uranium makes a lasting deal less likely before the new year.

State media framing · Tehran and Beijing

Tehran presents mining as enforcement of its own rules. Mehr calls the damaged ship a "non-compliant tanker", and Press TV says ships were "violating Iran's Hormuz transit regulations". IRNA calls the new sanctions proof of Washington's "failure" in the war. The F-16 claim is run as fact, with no mention of the US denial. The IRGC Aerospace commander promised "new surprises" after the chief visited an underground "missile city". Beijing's coverage is more measured. CGTN headlines "Iran signals openness to diplomacy as Trump rejects its terms", which puts the blame on Washington, and Xinhua carried CENTCOM's denial. Rybar, a Russian milblogger, dismissed the Hormuz "information wave" as "a bubble again".

Angle to build

Is Iran treating its Hormuz transit rules as a bargaining chip, or as a permanent new maritime order it wants recognised? If the latter, what does that mean for freedom-of-navigation norms Australia relies on in the South China Sea?

Iran-linked hackers targeted three Australian media figures, Erin Molan says

What happened
  • Broadcaster Erin Molan says she was targeted by a cyber-espionage operation in which someone posing as an expert from a US think tank invited her to an online meeting.
  • Molan says ASIO's Director-General told her she was one of three Australians targeted. Iran International names the other two as journalist Sharri Markson and commentator Michael Kroger.
  • Markson says Australian intelligence linked the operation to Charming Kitten (APT35), a group tied to the IRGC.
  • Molan: "I cannot be silenced."
  • Neither ASIO nor the government has publicly confirmed the attribution. The account comes from those targeted, via Iran International, an opposition outlet.
  • Context: Australia expelled Iran's ambassador in August 2025 after ASIO linked the IRGC to antisemitic arson attacks in Sydney and Melbourne.
Why it matters for Australia

This is foreign interference aimed directly at Australian public commentators, not at government networks. Impersonating think-tank experts is a well-known Charming Kitten method for harvesting credentials and contacts, and the people targeted may include academics and students in security studies. The case adds to the record of IRGC activity on Australian soil since the 2025 expulsion. It raises the question of whether Canberra will attribute it publicly or add to sanctions.

State media framing · Tehran

No Iranian state media statement on the Molan case was found. In the same period, Tehran Times attacked a British general's admission that UK hackers operate inside Iranian, Russian, Chinese and North Korean networks, calling it "shameless". This sets up a ready line for deflecting attribution: the West hacks too. Expect Tehran to dismiss any Australian attribution as Zionist-driven or as "Iranophobia".

Angle to build

Where is the line between espionage against journalists and coercion? Should Australia treat this as cyber espionage (normally left unattributed) or as foreign interference (which justifies public attribution and a response)?

The Baltic heats up: drones hit a Russian cargo ship as Denmark plot fallout spreads

What happened
  • On Saturday 21 November drones hit a Kaliningrad-bound cargo ship in international waters in the Gulf of Finland. Finland's Defence Forces say it caught fire west of Gogland island and drifted into Finnish waters. Leningrad's governor said two drones hit it about three nautical miles from the Russian border and that 80 drones were downed over the region. Ukraine had not commented.
  • Finland reimposed temporary air and sea restrictions in the eastern Gulf of Finland. PM Orpo said: "The war is spilling over into our neighbouring areas."
  • This follows the Ukrainian drone strike on the Russian corvette Naro-Fominsk near Gogland on 11–13 November.
  • Denmark is expelling Russia's defence attaché after two arrests over an alleged plot to kill a US-Ukrainian man. Von der Leyen called it "an attack on EU soil" and said the EU will propose a new European Security Strategy. Russia's embassy says the accusations are not backed by proof.
  • Estonia has reportedly closed its remaining border crossings with Russia. This was reported via TASS and not independently confirmed here.
  • Meanwhile Russia keeps striking Ukraine's grid, and the FT reports US negotiators want a Russia–Ukraine deal by early January, before Democrats take control of Congress.
Why it matters for Australia

Attacks on shipping near NATO coasts and alleged assassination plots in the EU test how far allies will go to defend against grey-zone operations, the same problem Australia faces with undersea cables and Pacific ports. A US-forced Ukraine deal on a political deadline would tell Indo-Pacific partners, Australia included, how Washington weighs a partner's sovereignty against a quick result.

State media framing · Moscow

Moscow frames itself as the victim. TASS reported the cargo ship strike, and Rybar cast the Baltic threat to Kaliningrad as coming "from so-called Ukraine". Zakharova said Estonia's border closure "will cost its initiators dearly" (translated from Russian). Rybar dismissed the Denmark case as "an assassination attempt without evidence". TASS commentators claim the UK, France and Germany helped plan the Crimean Bridge strike, a co-belligerence line. Dmitriev mocked Germany's Pistorius: "a vassal should not argue with its master". Xinhua covered the Denmark case neutrally and noted Russia's promised "appropriate response".

Angle to build

Ukraine is carrying its maritime drone war into the Baltic just as Washington pushes a deadline. Does this strengthen Kyiv's hand at the table, or give Moscow and parts of Washington a reason to blame Kyiv for escalation?

Shenzhen spin: Beijing and Moscow sell a "peace summit" that delivered little

What happened
  • Chinese and Russian state media are still pushing the 19 November Xi–Trump–Putin meeting in Shenzhen as historic.
  • Independent readings are cooler. The ABC's analysis says the declaration "delivered little in the way of concrete solutions", and Meduza's press review says "this was no Yalta". Trump says China reluctantly agreed to three-way nuclear risk-reduction talks.
  • SCMP, citing Treasury Secretary Bessent, reports that Xi and Trump agreed humans should always be involved in military AI decisions.
  • TASS says the three leaders agreed to jointly condemn "abuse of authority" by the International Criminal Court.
  • At the Manila Quad meeting Trump called Modi and Takaichi "tough" and told Albanese "we're OK too". The ABC says Albanese is still learning Trump's shifting rules.
  • Kim Jong Un passed up Trump's offer of talks during the regional trip and toured factories instead. Foreign reports of KCNA material say he again called North Korea's nuclear status irreversible.
Why it matters for Australia

A US president who praises great-power co-management with Beijing and Moscow raises a hard question for an ally: will issues such as Taiwan, the South China Sea or Ukraine be settled over the heads of middle powers? Three-way nuclear risk-reduction talks and a human-in-the-loop pledge on military AI would be modest gains for regional stability, if they hold.

State media framing · Beijing and Moscow

Xinhua calls the summit "historic for world stability, peace". People's Daily calls it a "peace summit", and CGTN runs its own poll claiming 88.3% support. Both lean on Trump's own words, "most momentous" and a "fantastic relationship", for credibility. China's Ministry of State Security says no nation can have "absolute security" at others' expense. RT calls the summit a "reality shock for the West" and dismisses critical coverage as "AI fakes". TASS amplifies claims of a "new global security architecture".

Angle to build

Is Beijing using Trump's validation to make the case to Asian audiences that US alliances matter less than great-power bargains? How should Canberra argue against that without seeming to oppose détente?
